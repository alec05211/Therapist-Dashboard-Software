# Session 05: Boundary backlash
# Approved production style: slow, uncertain, restrained, and reviewable

# This is synthetic demo material, not a real client record or clinical recommendation.

## Part 1 - The email after no

Jeremy: You look tired today.

Marcus: I slept about four hours. I did something healthy and then spent the night trying to undo it.

Jeremy: What did you do?

Marcus: My principal asked me to take on another student support group. Tuesdays after school, eight weeks. I said no.

Jeremy: How did you say it?

Marcus: "I do not have capacity to lead another group this term." Clear. Polite. Almost elegant.

Jeremy: And then?

Marcus: Three hours later I sent a five-paragraph email apologizing for my tone, explaining my caseload, offering to design the curriculum, and saying I could reconsider if they truly needed me.

Jeremy: What happened in those three hours?

Marcus: Nothing happened. That was the problem. She said, "I understand," and walked away.

Jeremy: Her response was not angry.

Marcus: Not visibly. But it was short. I replayed her face until I was certain I had disappointed her.

Jeremy: What did the apology email protect you from?

Marcus: The possibility that she thought I was unhelpful. [pause] It also made the no basically meaningless.

## Part 2 - The backlash inside

Jeremy: When you first said no, what did you notice?

Marcus: Relief. Immediate, physical relief. My shoulders dropped. Then about thirty seconds later, heat climbed up my neck.

Jeremy: What did the heat mean to you?

Marcus: Danger. I had broken character. Marcus is the person who says, "I can make that work."

Jeremy: What story followed?

Marcus: That the principal would stop trusting me. That when budgets get cut, she would remember I was not a team player. That the students would lose a group because I wanted to protect my Tuesday evenings.

Jeremy: Did she say any of that?

Marcus: No.

Jeremy: How certain did it feel?

Marcus: Completely certain. [pause] I know how that sounds.

Jeremy: It sounds like an alarm, not a fact.

Marcus: The alarm was loud enough that facts did not matter. By midnight I had rewritten the email fourteen times.

Jeremy: What did you hope the final version would accomplish?

Marcus: Make her like me again.

Jeremy: Had she stopped liking you?

Marcus: [long pause] I do not know. Probably not.

## Part 3 - Boundary or rejection

Jeremy: What is a boundary, in your own words?

Marcus: A limit on what I can do without damaging myself or doing bad work.

Jeremy: And what is rejection?

Marcus: Telling someone they do not matter. Leaving them alone with a problem.

Jeremy: Are those the same action?

Marcus: No. They feel identical in my body.

Jeremy: Could we slow that down? When you declined the group, what did you reject?

Marcus: The extra assignment.

Jeremy: Did you reject your principal?

Marcus: No.

Jeremy: The students?

Marcus: No. I already meet with twenty-seven students every week.

Jeremy: Then the boundary may have protected your ability to keep showing up for the work you already accepted.

Marcus: [pause] That sounds reasonable when you say it here. At school it felt selfish.

Jeremy: A feeling can be important information without being a verdict.

Marcus: So guilt does not automatically mean I did something wrong.

Jeremy: Would it fit better to treat guilt as something to examine?

Marcus: Yes. Evidence to review, not a conviction.

## Part 4 - The cost of erasing the limit

Jeremy: What was the effect of the follow-up email?

Marcus: My principal replied this morning. She thanked me for the curriculum offer and said we could discuss it Friday.

Jeremy: How did you feel reading that?

Marcus: Trapped. Which is unfair, because I reopened the door.

Jeremy: You tried to reduce one discomfort and created another.

Marcus: Exactly. I could not tolerate her possible disappointment, so I volunteered future Marcus to clean it up.

Jeremy: What does future Marcus need from you now?

Marcus: A repair. But not another dramatic apology.

Jeremy: What would you want the repair to preserve?

Marcus: The original limit. I do not want to lead the group. I also do not want to pretend the email never happened.

Jeremy: Could we draft something brief?

Marcus: Maybe. [pause] "I want to clarify my note from yesterday. I can share the materials I already have, but I am not available to design or lead the group this term. I appreciate your understanding."

Jeremy: How does that land?

Marcus: Firm. A little cold.

Jeremy: What would make it warm without erasing the limit?

Marcus: "I hope the existing materials are useful." That is enough. I do not need to explain my whole nervous system.

## Part 5 - Practicing the repair

Jeremy: Would you like to rehearse the Friday conversation too?

Marcus: Yes, although every part of me wants to call in sick.

Jeremy: I will be your principal. "Marcus, I was hoping you might reconsider. The students respond well to you."

Marcus: [pause] "I care about the students, and I am not available to lead another group this term. I can send the materials by Monday."

Jeremy: "Could you at least get it started for the first two weeks?"

Marcus: [long pause] "No, I cannot commit to that."

Jeremy: What happened just then?

Marcus: My stomach dropped, and I wanted to add six sentences.

Jeremy: Can we stay with the drop for a moment?

Marcus: [long pause] It rises, then settles. I am still here. You are still here.

Jeremy: Shall we try again?

Marcus: "I cannot lead or launch the group this term. I can share the materials I already have."

Jeremy: Clear and respectful.

Marcus: It feels almost rude because there is no apology.

Jeremy: Respect does not require self-erasure.

Marcus: I want to write that down.

## Part 6 - Keeping the boundary after the room

Jeremy: What usually happens after a conversation like that?

Marcus: I leave the room, imagine ten different versions of what she thinks, then send another email.

Jeremy: What could support you during the hour after Friday's meeting?

Marcus: I could walk outside instead of going straight to my laptop. Text Andre that I did it. Wait until Saturday before sending anything else.

Jeremy: And if there is a genuine factual correction?

Marcus: Write it down, review it the next day, and keep it to three sentences. No midnight autobiography.

Jeremy: What do you expect the guilt to do?

Marcus: Get loud. Tell me I am abandoning people.

Jeremy: What might you say back?

Marcus: "A limit is not a rejection. Discomfort is not proof of harm." [pause] And maybe, "Future Marcus gets a vote."

Jeremy: What does future Marcus want Tuesday evening for?

Marcus: Dinner with Andre. Sleep. Maybe nothing. I keep trying to justify rest with a better activity, but maybe an unscheduled evening is allowed.

Jeremy: Does that feel possible this week?

Marcus: Possible, not comfortable.

Jeremy: Is comfort the requirement?

Marcus: No. [long pause] The requirement is that I stop turning every no into a yes with extra steps.

Jeremy: What will you do first?

Marcus: Send the clarification tomorrow morning, then close the laptop. No second email.

Jeremy: And if you feel the urge?

Marcus: Let the song finish. Let the feeling finish too.

Jeremy: That sounds connected to last week.

Marcus: It is. I can survive a feeling without assigning it a task.

Jeremy: Is that a good place to stop?

Marcus: Yes. Before I add six more sentences.
