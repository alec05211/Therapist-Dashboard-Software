## Part 1 - Anthony handled it

Jeremy: How did Saturday go?

Marcus: [pause] The house is still standing. The furnace works. Anthony showed up.

Jeremy: And you stayed in Pennsylvania?

Marcus: Technically. I did not cross a state line. I did, however, wake up at six and stare at my phone like it was a medical monitor.

Jeremy: Did you call him?

Marcus: I wrote four texts and deleted all four. At ten-twelve he sent a picture of the technician in the basement. No caption. Just a man in coveralls holding a wrench.

Jeremy: What did you notice when you saw it?

Marcus: Relief first. Then irritation that he had not told me the technician arrived. Then embarrassment that I was irritated. [pause] Anthony called at eleven-thirty. He had the receipt, the warranty, everything. He even asked about the rattling pipe I forgot to mention.

Jeremy: How was it to hear that?

Marcus: Good. Uncomfortable. He sounded proud of himself, and I realized I almost kept him from having that.

Jeremy: What did you say?

Marcus: I said, "Thank you. You really helped Dad." I did not correct the way he filed the receipt.

Jeremy: That sounds deliberate.

Marcus: It was exhausting. [pause] But then something else happened Sunday.

## Part 2 - The song in the kitchen

Jeremy: What happened Sunday?

Marcus: I was making coffee. Andre had gone to the gym, and I put on an old soul playlist. "Ain't No Mountain High Enough" came on.

Jeremy: What did that bring up?

Marcus: My mother. She used to play it on Saturday mornings while she cleaned the kitchen. Not sing it exactly. More like shout half the words and make up the rest.

Jeremy: What did you remember?

Marcus: Her yellow robe. The sleeve always dipped into the dishwater. She would snap the towel at us if we tried to steal bacon before breakfast. [pause] I could see the sunlight on the table. I could smell Pine-Sol and coffee.

Jeremy: What happened in your body as the memory arrived?

Marcus: My throat closed. I turned toward the speaker to shut it off. My hand was already out.

Jeremy: Did you turn it off?

Marcus: No. [long pause] I left it playing.

Jeremy: What made you leave it?

Marcus: I heard your voice asking if I could let eighty percent be enough. It was not the same thing, but I thought maybe I did not have to control this either. Maybe the song could finish.

Jeremy: And while it finished?

Marcus: I sat on the kitchen floor. I cried hard enough that I scared myself.

## Part 3 - Grief without a task

Jeremy: What was frightening about crying that hard?

Marcus: There was no job attached to it. No form, no phone call, no person to calm down. It was just missing her. [long pause] I kept saying, "I want my mother," which is not a sentence a thirty-eight-year-old man expects to hear himself say.

Jeremy: What is it like to say that here?

Marcus: Humiliating. True. Both.

Jeremy: I do not hear anything childish in missing your mother.

Marcus: My mind knows that. The rest of me thinks grief should be quieter by now.

Jeremy: Who taught you that grief has a schedule?

Marcus: [pause] Nobody said it directly. People praised me for going back to work. They said Mom would be proud that I was holding everyone together.

Jeremy: Praise can become an instruction.

Marcus: Yes. Keep moving. Do not make anyone worry. Be the capable version.

Jeremy: On the kitchen floor, were you incapable?

Marcus: [long pause] No. I was sad. That is different, I guess.

Jeremy: Does the distinction fit?

Marcus: It does. I hate how much it fits.

## Part 4 - Affection beside pain

Jeremy: When you remember the kitchen now, is it only painful?

Marcus: No. That is the confusing part. I was crying, but I was also laughing because she truly did not know the second verse. She would point the wooden spoon at us like we were backup singers who had failed her.

Jeremy: Grief and affection were there together.

Marcus: Yes. I usually treat the good memories like traps. If I let one in, the hospital comes right behind it.

Jeremy: Did the hospital come this time?

Marcus: It tried. I saw the fluorescent hallway for a second. Then I looked at the coffee mug in my hand. It was hers, the blue one with the chipped rim. I stayed with the kitchen.

Jeremy: You chose where to place your attention.

Marcus: Not perfectly. But I did not turn the memory into a review of what I should have done differently.

Jeremy: What did the memory give you when it was allowed to remain unfinished and uncorrected?

Marcus: [long pause] Her laugh. The way she filled a room. I miss her, but for a few minutes she was more than the person who died in a hospital. She was my mother making terrible music before breakfast.

Jeremy: What do you notice now?

Marcus: My chest hurts. But it is a softer hurt.

## Part 5 - Sharing the memory

Jeremy: Did you tell anyone what happened?

Marcus: Andre came home and found me on the floor. My first instinct was to stand up and say I dropped something.

Jeremy: What did you do instead?

Marcus: I told him the song got me. He sat down beside me. He did not ask questions. He just leaned against the cabinet.

Jeremy: How was it to let him see you there?

Marcus: Exposed. Then relieved. [pause] Later I texted Maya and Anthony the name of the song. Maya sent three crying emojis. Anthony wrote, "Yellow robe Saturday," which made me laugh again.

Jeremy: The memory belonged to all three of you.

Marcus: Exactly. I have been guarding her like the memories are records only I can maintain. But they remember things I do not.

Jeremy: What might change if remembering her becomes shared work?

Marcus: I would not have to carry the whole archive. [pause] There is a family dinner next month for Dad's birthday. I have already made the restaurant list, called about the private room, and started a spreadsheet.

Jeremy: Of course you have.

Marcus: I know. [small laugh] Anthony offered to choose the restaurant last night. I almost said the options were complicated.

## Part 6 - Letting his brother lead

Jeremy: What did you say to Anthony?

Marcus: I said, "Yes. Please choose it and make the reservation." Then I added six conditions.

Jeremy: Six?

Marcus: Parking, stairs, noise, Dad's blood pressure, Maya's class schedule, and whether they split checks.

Jeremy: Is there room to revise the request?

Marcus: I could call him and say, "You know Dad. Pick somewhere he will enjoy. I trust you."

Jeremy: What feeling comes with that sentence?

Marcus: Anxiety. Also relief. I do trust him. I just do not trust my ability to tolerate not knowing every detail.

Jeremy: Those are different problems.

Marcus: Right. Anthony choosing a restaurant is not dangerous. My discomfort is not evidence that the plan is failing.

Jeremy: What would make this an experiment rather than a test he can pass or fail?

Marcus: I do not check the reservation. I show up. If the table is noisy, we ask to move. If parking is bad, we walk an extra block. Nothing collapses.

Jeremy: And if you feel the urge to take over?

Marcus: I can remember the picture of the technician. Anthony handled it. Different from me is not the same as careless.

Jeremy: What do you want to carry from the kitchen memory this week?

Marcus: [long pause] That loving my mother does not require me to turn every memory into a responsibility. Sometimes the song can just finish.

Jeremy: Is that enough for today?

Marcus: Yes. [pause] More than enough.
