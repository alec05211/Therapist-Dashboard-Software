## Part 1 - The weekend call

Jeremy: Good morning, Marcus. How are you stepping into today?

Marcus: [pause] Better than I felt on Saturday. Saturday was rough.

Jeremy: What happened on Saturday?

Marcus: Before we get to Saturday, I should say that the night experiment actually did something. On Thursday, I woke up at three-twenty. The usual chest hit, the adrenaline. But instead of grabbing the notebook or having an argument with myself, I just laid there and said, "The guard woke up."

Jeremy: What did that feel like?

Marcus: Strange. Like giving a glass of water to someone who is out of breath. It did not magically put me to sleep, but I got up, sat in my chair in the living room with a blanket, and drank some water. I was not furious at my own pulse for once.

Jeremy: That sounds like a meaningful pause.

Marcus: It was. And then Friday evening came, and my family completely dismantled my calm. [pause] Or I dismantled it.

Jeremy: Tell me about Friday evening.

Marcus: My sister Maya called around seven. My father's furnace in Baltimore has been making this awful rattling noise, and the pilot light keeps going out. Winter is coming, so it needs to be fixed. Maya said the contractor gave an estimate that did not make sense, and Dad was getting upset.

Jeremy: What did Maya ask of you?

Marcus: She said, "Marcus, Dad is not listening to me. He says the man is trying to rip him off. Can you call the company tomorrow morning and sort it out? He will only listen to you."

Jeremy: And what happened inside you when she asked?

Marcus: [long pause] A spike of fury. Pure resentment. My immediate thought was, "Why is this on me? Why can nobody in this family solve a basic problem without calling Philadelphia?"

Jeremy: That was the first reaction.

Marcus: Yes. It hit me right in the chest. And then, about three seconds later, I felt completely sick with myself.

## Part 2 - The reflex to agree

Jeremy: What brought on the sick feeling?

Marcus: Guilt. Instant, suffocating guilt. Maya is working thirty-five hours a week at the clinic and taking classes at night. My dad is seventy-one and grieving his wife. And here I am, sitting in my comfortable apartment, getting angry because my family needs my help.

Jeremy: What did you tell yourself in that moment?

Marcus: That I was selfish. That my mother would be appalled if she heard me thinking like that. She spent thirty years making sure everybody was fed and taken care of, and I cannot even make a phone call about a boiler without throwing a tantrum in my own head.

Jeremy: What did you actually say to Maya?

Marcus: "Of course. Do not worry about it, Maya. Send me the paperwork, and I will call them first thing in the morning." [pause] My voice was perfectly calm. Smooth as glass.

Jeremy: While inside your stomach was screaming.

Marcus: Exactly. I said yes before she could even take another breath.

Jeremy: What would have happened if you had paused before answering?

Marcus: [long pause] She would have heard the hesitation. And if she heard the hesitation, she would think I did not care. Or she would feel bad for asking, and then I would feel worse for making her feel bad.

Jeremy: So the instant agreement protects her from feeling like a burden.

Marcus: Yes.

Jeremy: And who does it protect Marcus from?

Marcus: [pause] It protects me from being the son or the brother who said no. If I say yes, I am still the person who holds the family together.

Jeremy: Even if it costs you your weekend.

Marcus: Even if it costs me my weekend. I spent four hours on Saturday tracking down parts, calling the heating company, and walking my dad through every line on the estimate. By two o'clock, I had a migraine so bad I could barely see out of my left eye.

## Part 3 - Indispensable

Jeremy: You did the job. The furnace got handled.

Marcus: It did. The technician is scheduled for this coming Saturday. Dad is calm. Maya sent me a heart emoji. Everyone is relieved.

Jeremy: Except you.

Marcus: [pause] Except me. I spent the rest of the weekend feeling exhausted, irritable, and angry at everyone. And then angry at myself for being angry at them. It is an endless loop.

Jeremy: How long has that loop been running, Marcus?

Marcus: Since I was twelve. When my parents fought about money, I was the one who kept my brother and sister in the back bedroom playing card games so they would not hear. When forms came home from school, I filled them out myself and brought them to my mother to sign.

Jeremy: Being dependable started very early.

Marcus: It was how I was useful. In our house, if you were calm and handled your business, nobody had to worry about you.

Jeremy: I wonder if being indispensable protects you from something.

Marcus: [pause] Protects me? How does spending four hours on a Saturday arguing with an HVAC supplier in Baltimore protect me? It wipes me out. It takes away the only free time I have all week.

Jeremy: It certainly drains you.

Marcus: Then how is that protection? It feels like a sentence.

Jeremy: Because when you are the one holding the steering wheel, you never have to wonder where the car is going. And you never have to ask anyone else to drive.

Marcus: [long pause] That... [pause] I want to argue with that.

Jeremy: You can argue with it. Take your time.

Marcus: [long pause] If I do not hold the wheel, the car goes off the road. That is what it feels like.

## Part 4 - The risk of needing others

Jeremy: What would happen if the car went off the road?

Marcus: My father would get overcharged a thousand dollars he does not have. Or the furnace would quit in the middle of January and he would sit in a freezing house because he is too proud to admit he cannot afford the emergency rate.

Jeremy: Those are real concerns. But you also have a brother and a sister.

Marcus: Maya is swamped. And my brother Anthony... [pause] Anthony is twenty-nine. He works at a logistics warehouse in Dundalk. He lives twenty minutes from my dad's house.

Jeremy: Why was Anthony not the one to handle the furnace?

Marcus: [long pause] Anthony does not follow up. He will say he is going to do it, and then he gets busy, or he forgets to ask for the warranty, or he agrees to whatever the repairman says without reading the fine print.

Jeremy: So he might not do it the way you would do it.

Marcus: He definitely would not do it the way I would do it. And then I would have to go in and fix what he messed up anyway.

Jeremy: What is the risk for you in asking Anthony for help?

Marcus: [pause] That he will let me down.

Jeremy: And if he lets you down?

Marcus: Then I am stuck dealing with the problem and the disappointment at the same time. [long pause] If I just do it myself from the start, there is no disappointment. There is just the work.

Jeremy: The work is painful, but predictable.

Marcus: Yes. Predictable pain is easier to manage than unexpected failure. If I depend on myself, I know the limits. If I depend on somebody else, I am exposed.

Jeremy: Needing someone means giving up the illusion of control.

Marcus: [long pause] It means trusting that someone cares enough to hold their end of the rope. And if they let go... you fall.

## Part 5 - Care versus control

Jeremy: When your mother was in the hospital, who held the rope?

Marcus: I did. I had the binder. The medical history, the lab results, the doctors' names. Anthony and Maya came to visit, but they sat in the chairs. I was the one standing by the bed talking to the attending physician.

Jeremy: Did you invite them to stand by the bed?

Marcus: [long pause] I... I do not know. I think I thought I was protecting them. Maya was crying so much. Anthony gets quiet and shuts down when he is overwhelmed. I thought, "I am the counselor. I can handle doctors. I can handle hard news."

Jeremy: And looking back now?

Marcus: [pause] Maybe I did not give them room. Maybe I took over because if I was managing the charts, I did not have to feel how terrified I was.

Jeremy: Control gave you something to do with the terror.

Marcus: Yes. But it also kept them at a distance. And now they look at me like I am the only one who knows how to keep the house standing. Maya calls me because I trained her to call me.

Jeremy: That is a significant realization.

Marcus: It feels awful to admit. I spent the last three days being resentful that my sister treats me like an emergency service, when I am the one who built the system that made her do it.

Jeremy: Could we look at that with some compassion rather than using it as another stick to beat yourself with?

Marcus: [pause] Compassion is not my strongest subject.

Jeremy: You built that system out of deep love for your family and a desperate desire to protect your mother. It was not malicious. But the system is costing you more than you can afford to pay right now.

Marcus: [long pause] I cannot keep paying it. I am running out of credit.

## Part 6 - Tolerating the discomfort

Jeremy: The technician is coming this Saturday to your father's house. What was your original plan?

Marcus: I was going to wake up at five-thirty, drive two hours down Interstate 95 to Baltimore, stand in the basement with the repairman, pay the deposit, check the thermostat, and drive two hours back home.

Jeremy: A four-hour round trip for a ninety-minute repair.

Marcus: It sounds ridiculous when you say the numbers out loud.

Jeremy: What would happen if you did not drive down on Saturday?

Marcus: [pause] Someone has to be there to let the technician in and make sure he replaces the blower motor like we agreed.

Jeremy: Who lives twenty minutes from your father's house?

Marcus: Anthony.

Jeremy: What would it take to ask Anthony to be there?

Marcus: [long pause] A lot of deep breathing. I would have to call him. Not text him. Call him and say, "Anthony, I cannot drive down this weekend. Can you go over to Dad's house at ten o'clock and meet the repairman?"

Jeremy: And what would happen if he said yes?

Marcus: I would spend all Saturday morning staring at the clock, wondering if he showed up on time, wondering if he checked the receipt, wanting to call him thirty times.

Jeremy: Can you tolerate Anthony doing the job his way? Even if it is eighty percent of what you would do?

Marcus: [pause] Eighty percent feels like a D-minus to me, Jeremy.

Jeremy: What if eighty percent is enough to fix the furnace?

Marcus: [long pause] If the heat turns on, Dad is safe. The house is warm. Even if Anthony forgets to ask for the itemized warranty.

Jeremy: Can you let eighty percent be enough this Saturday?

Marcus: I can try. I will call Anthony tonight. I will write down three bullet points for him. Just three. And then I will stay in Pennsylvania.

Jeremy: And when the anxiety spikes on Saturday morning?

Marcus: I will remind myself that letting Anthony step up is not neglect. It is giving my brother a chance to be a brother. [pause] And giving myself permission to be a human being for one afternoon.

Jeremy: That sounds like a powerful step. How does it feel to name that?

Marcus: [long pause] Terrifying. But like I can finally take a full breath.

Jeremy: We will see how Saturday goes. Thank you, Marcus.

Marcus: Thank you, Jeremy.
