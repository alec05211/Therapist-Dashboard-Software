## Part 1 - Returning

Jeremy: Welcome back, Marcus. How was it walking back into the building today?

Marcus: [pause] A little strange. Better than last week. I did not sit in the car for twenty minutes trying to talk myself out of the elevator.

Jeremy: That sounds like a shift.

Marcus: It was. I still sat there for five minutes, but... mostly just to make sure I had my keys and my phone. Not because I was planning an escape.

Jeremy: How did the rest of the week sit with you after our first conversation?

Marcus: Heavy at first. When I left here, I felt like someone had peeled back a layer of paint and I was walking around in the wind. By Thursday it settled back into the usual routine.

Jeremy: Did you send the message to Andre?

Marcus: [pause] I did. Right from the parking lot, before I could talk myself out of it. I wrote: "Can I tell you something without you trying to fix it?"

Jeremy: What happened?

Marcus: He called within three minutes. He said, "Who is hurt?" [pause] I almost laughed, but then I told him about the grocery store. The real version. The chest tightness, leaving the cart, sitting on the curb.

Jeremy: How was that to tell him?

Marcus: Humiliating for about thirty seconds. Then he just stayed on the line. He said, "Man, why didn't you call me while you were sitting on the curb?" [long pause] I told him I did not want to be a project. He told me to shut up and stop being an idiot. Which is his way of saying he cares.

Jeremy: It sounds like he met you with care.

Marcus: He did. I still felt an urge ten minutes later to text him back and say, "Everything is fine now, do not worry." But I remembered what we said. I put the phone in the glove compartment and drove home.

Jeremy: That took restraint.

Marcus: It felt unnatural. Like leaving a bill unpaid.

Jeremy: And what about the three in the morning experiment?

Marcus: [pause] Well. That is where my habits showed up in full force.

Jeremy: How so?

Marcus: You suggested writing down the one thing I was afraid I would forget. Just one thing, no score attached. [pause] I stopped at a pharmacy on the way home, bought a leather notebook and three different pens, and spent forty minutes organizing sections. Work. Dad. Maya. Household.

Jeremy: You built a curriculum.

Marcus: I built a syllabus. [long pause] I got up at three-fifteen on Tuesday, looked at the notebook, and realized I had turned sleeping into homework.

## Part 2 - Mapping the alarm

Jeremy: Even when the invitation was to do less, the instinct was to make it an assignment.

Marcus: If it is an assignment, I know how to get an A. If it is just sitting there with an empty page at three in the morning... I do not know what I am supposed to produce.

Jeremy: What if you did not have to produce anything?

Marcus: [long pause] That sentence gives me a physical reaction in my stomach. It feels reckless.

Jeremy: Let us stay with that physical reaction for a moment. When you wake up at three or three-fifteen, what actually happens in your body?

Marcus: It is instantaneous. There is no in-between. I do not drift awake or wonder what time it is. My eyes open, and I am already at ninety miles an hour.

Jeremy: What do you notice first?

Marcus: My chest. My heart is beating hard, like I have been jogging. My jaw is clenched so tight my teeth ache. My shoulders are pulled up toward my ears.

Jeremy: And your mind?

Marcus: Immediately cataloging. Did I submit the quarterly report for the district? Did my sister call the plumber for my dad's hot water heater? Did I leave the oven on? [pause] Things that are not even plausible. My brain is searching for whatever justifies the adrenaline.

Jeremy: It sounds like the physical alarm goes off first, and then the mind scrambles to find a reason for it.

Marcus: Exactly. Like someone pulled the fire alarm in an empty auditorium, and the security guard starts running around looking for smoke until he finds a dusty lightbulb and blames that.

Jeremy: What does your body think the danger is?

Marcus: [pause] That something is happening right now, in this second, that I am failing to prevent.

Jeremy: That is a heavy burden to wake up to.

Marcus: It is exhausting. I lie there in the dark and tell myself, "Marcus, you are thirty-four years old. You are in your bed in Havertown. The door is deadbolted. Go to sleep." But my body does not believe me.

Jeremy: Rational reassurance does not seem to reach where the alarm is sounding.

Marcus: No. It feels like arguing with a siren.

## Part 3 - The night shift

Jeremy: If rational reassurance cannot quiet the alarm, I wonder where the siren learned its schedule.

Marcus: What do you mean?

Jeremy: Three in the morning is specific. Has there been another time in your life when three in the morning required you to be alert?

Marcus: [long pause] Baltimore.

Jeremy: Tell me about Baltimore.

Marcus: When my mother was sick. The last six months. She was at home for most of it, before the final hospitalization. My father was exhausted. He drove a bus for thirty-two years; he knows how to keep a schedule, but managing four different medications and oxygen levels terrified him.

Jeremy: So you took on the night watch.

Marcus: I would drive down on Friday after school, stay through Sunday, and during the week I had my phone on my pillow. Not on the nightstand. On the pillow, right next to my ear.

Jeremy: With the ringer on.

Marcus: Maximum volume. I set a specific chime for my father and my sister so I would know if it was an emergency before I even opened my eyes. [pause] And almost every night, around three or three-thirty, I would wake up anyway.

Jeremy: Why then?

Marcus: That was when her pain medication would start to wear off. My father would wake up confused, or she would have trouble breathing. If the phone rang, I had to be awake enough to tell him which bottle to look for, or whether to call the visiting nurse.

Jeremy: You could not afford to be groggy.

Marcus: No. Groggy meant a mistake. Groggy meant my mother suffered or my father panicked. [long pause] So I trained myself to go from zero to fully awake in two seconds.

Jeremy: Sleep was not safe.

Marcus: Sleep was dangerous. Sleeping meant leaving the post unguarded.

## Part 4 - Standing down

Jeremy: For six months, your body learned that survival, her comfort and your father's stability, depended on you waking up instantly at three in the morning.

Marcus: [pause] Yes.

Jeremy: And eleven months ago, your mother died.

Marcus: [long pause] Yes.

Jeremy: What did anyone say to your body about the post?

Marcus: [pause] Nothing. [long pause] Nobody said anything.

Jeremy: You organized the funeral. You supported your siblings. You checked on your father. And then you went back to work.

Marcus: On Tuesday. The funeral was Saturday. I was in my office on Tuesday morning reviewing senior transcripts.

Jeremy: So the post was never closed. The guard was never relieved of duty.

Marcus: [pause] It sounds ridiculous when you say it like that. I know she is gone. I know the phone is not going to ring with a nurse on the line. I know that in my head every single day.

Jeremy: But bodies do not read death certificates, Marcus. Your body learned a reflex of vigilance that kept people you love safe. It has been running that drill every night because it believes that stopping means disaster.

Marcus: [long pause] When I wake up at three-fifteen now, it is almost like... if I go back to sleep easily, it feels like I am forgetting her.

Jeremy: Say more about that.

Marcus: [pause] As long as I am on edge, as long as I am carrying the list, it feels like I am still taking care of her. Or taking care of what she left behind. If I just roll over and sleep eight hours like nothing happened, it feels... callous.

Jeremy: So the insomnia is not just an old reflex. It has also become a form of loyalty.

Marcus: [long pause] That is painful to hear. But I think it is true.

## Part 5 - The quiet apartment

Jeremy: How does the evening feel before you go to bed? What happens between leaving the school and getting under the covers?

Marcus: I come home around five-thirty or six. I walk into the apartment. It is completely quiet. [pause] After eight hours of teenagers shouting in hallways and guidance meetings and ringing bells, the silence is almost aggressive.

Jeremy: What do you do with that silence?

Marcus: I try to fill it, or I try to conquer it. I put on a podcast. I make dinner. Then by nine o'clock, I start my wind-down routine.

Jeremy: You say that with a certain tone.

Marcus: Because it is not winding down. It is another operational procedure. I brew chamomile tea. I turn off the overhead lights. I put my phone across the room. I open a meditation app.

Jeremy: And how does that feel?

Marcus: Tense. I am sitting on the edge of the mattress drinking tea like it is medicine, looking at the clock thinking, "You have six hours and forty minutes until the alarm. You need to relax right now. Why are you not relaxed?"

Jeremy: Demanding rest with a clenched fist.

Marcus: Exactly. I am threatening myself with self-care. "Breathe deeply or tomorrow is going to be ruined."

Jeremy: What is it like to notice that?

Marcus: It makes me realize that I treat rest like a job performance. If I do not fall asleep within twenty minutes, I have failed the rubric.

Jeremy: What might happen if there were no rubric at night?

Marcus: [pause] I think I am afraid of what would happen if I stopped managing everything. If I am not organizing the night, who is?

Jeremy: And what if the night does not need to be organized?

Marcus: [long pause] That sounds like a language I do not speak yet.

## Part 6 - Relieving the guard

Jeremy: As we think about the coming week, what would an experiment look like that does not turn into another syllabus?

Marcus: First of all, please do not give me a checklist. If you tell me to do ten minutes of journaling and fifteen minutes of stretching, I will buy a timer and grade myself on my posture.

Jeremy: No checklists. No timers. No grades.

Marcus: Thank you. [pause] I need that boundary.

Jeremy: What if the goal for this week is not to fix the waking at three in the morning?

Marcus: [long pause] That feels strange to hear from a therapist. If we are not trying to fix it, what are we doing?

Jeremy: What if we acknowledged that your body is waking up for a reason that made complete sense? That the guard is waking up out of love and habit, not because you are broken.

Marcus: [pause] How would I do that at three in the morning without making it a chore?

Jeremy: When your eyes snap open and you feel that rush of adrenaline, what if instead of grabbing a notebook or ordering yourself to sleep, you took one breath and said inside: "The guard woke up. The emergency is over, but thank you for watching."

Marcus: [pause] Say thank you to the alarm?

Jeremy: To the part of you that spent six months keeping your mother safe and your father from falling apart. It learned its job too well. You do not have to fight it.

Marcus: And then what? Do I stay in bed?

Jeremy: If you are awake, you are awake. You could get up, sit in the living room chair with a blanket, drink a glass of water, or listen to music. No goal of getting back to sleep by a certain time. Just sitting with the quiet.

Marcus: [long pause] Telling the guard he can take a seat.

Jeremy: Telling the guard he has done his duty.

Marcus: [pause] That feels... different. A little scary. But a lot less exhausting than lying there furious at my own heart rate.

Jeremy: We can see what happens. Once or twice this week. If it happens, fine. If you forget and get frustrated, that is fine too.

Marcus: No score.

Jeremy: No score. How does it feel to end here today?

Marcus: [long pause] Quieter. Like someone turned down the volume on the static just a little bit.

Jeremy: That is a good place to pause. Thank you, Marcus.

Marcus: Thank you, Jeremy. See you next week.
