## Part 1
THERAPIST: [warm, grounded, ordinary conversational pace] You said in your message that the meeting happened and nobody left early.

AVERY: That was the most positive sentence I could verify. Nobody left. Nobody threw anything. Dad ate two pieces of coffee cake while we discussed whether he should be allowed to receive his own mail, which made the whole thing feel both serious and ridiculous.

THERAPIST: Where do you want to begin?

AVERY: With the chairs, because apparently symbolic seating did not save us. I sat beside Daniel like we discussed. Mia noticed and said, "Oh, are we mixing the teams?" I said there were no teams. Daniel said, "That sounds like something a team captain says." Dad laughed. I did not.

THERAPIST: What was happening for you then?

AVERY: I had the index card in my pocket. The spreadsheet was in my bag by the door. I had slept badly and rehearsed the opening in the shower. Their joking felt like they were puncturing the meeting before it started. But Dad looked less tense after he laughed, so I let it go.

THERAPIST: Did you use the opening you practiced?

AVERY: Almost exactly. I said, "I want Dad to keep making his decisions, and I want the work around those decisions to be more predictable. I cannot continue as the default for every call, appointment change, and backup." Then I stopped.

THERAPIST: How did the stop go?

AVERY: [dry, self-aware] Four seconds. A personal record. Mia looked at the table. Daniel rubbed his forehead. Dad said, "I did not know there was a default." That was the first edge.

THERAPIST: What did you say?

AVERY: "There is. It has mostly been me." Dad said, "Mostly because you answer." Daniel made a sound—not a word, just agreement through his nose. I felt heat from my collar to my face.

THERAPIST: What did you do with the heat?

AVERY: I gripped the card in my pocket hard enough to bend it. I said, "That is partly true. It is also true that the clinic, pharmacy, and all of you contact me first." Mia said, "I do not contact you first every time." I said, "This is exactly why I made—" and stopped before I said spreadsheet.

THERAPIST: What helped you stop?

AVERY: Leah's phrase: do not put the machine in the center. I said, "I am not asking us to agree on every past call. I am asking us to assign three current areas." Daniel said, "Okay, then ask." His tone was impatient, but he was right.

THERAPIST: Which request came first?

AVERY: Medication backup. I described Mia contacting Daniel directly by Wednesday if she could not pick up Thursday, then posting to the group if neither could. Mia said yes before I finished. Daniel said, "I can be backup when I am off, not automatic backup." I said the role was to respond and help identify the option, not always drive.

THERAPIST: How did he take that?

AVERY: He asked if I had already written the workflow. Mia laughed again. I said, "I wrote the request." Dad said, "Of course she did." There was affection in his voice, I think. There was also a whole family story about me being the person with paper.

THERAPIST: Were you able to stay with the current request?

AVERY: Yes. Barely. Daniel agreed to answer by Wednesday evening when Mia flags a problem. He would either cover, suggest delivery, or say he could not. That is a real agreement. It is not the same as owning every backup, but it gets me out of being the private dispatcher.

THERAPIST: How did that first agreement feel?

AVERY: Smaller than I wanted and more solid than I expected.

THERAPIST: Did anyone write it down then?

AVERY: Mia wrote it on the paper bag from the bakery because my index card was still in my pocket and I was trying not to become secretary. Daniel said, "Can we use a normal piece of paper?" Dad said the bag was already there. For five minutes the plan lived under a grease mark from the coffee cake. I had to work very hard not to transfer it into a formatted document while people were still talking.

THERAPIST: What did you do instead?

AVERY: I asked Mia to read it back. She left out the Wednesday evening deadline. I corrected that because the deadline is functional, not because the handwriting bothered me. Daniel agreed. Dad asked why Wednesday mattered if pickup was Thursday, and we explained the pharmacy hours. It was slow. Nobody died from the inefficiency.

THERAPIST: Was your father participating or being informed?

AVERY: Participating. He objected to the phrase "backup chain" because it sounded mechanical. He suggested "who to call next." That is what the bag says. His phrase is clearer. I would never have chosen it because it is not technically a chain, and that may be exactly why he should have words in the plan.

## Part 2
THERAPIST: [clarifying, low-interruption] What happened when you moved to transportation?

AVERY: That is where the room got sharp. I asked Daniel to own arranging two routine rides a month. He said, "Why two?" I said because the ledger showed an average of four or five and I could do one, Mia could sometimes do one, and two needed another owner. He heard the word ledger immediately.

THERAPIST: What did he say?

AVERY: "You did make a case file." Mia said, "Avery." Dad asked to see it. I said it was a private tracking document, not the agenda. Daniel said, "Private except it is determining my quota." I told him the number came from actual appointments, not a character judgment.

THERAPIST: And he responded?

AVERY: He said his shifts rotate, he cannot promise two rides without dates, and he is already handling repairs. I said arranging did not always mean driving. He said, "So you are giving me the exact phone-tree job you hate and calling that sharing."

THERAPIST: Did that land?

AVERY: [measured, frustrated] It did. I had anticipated it here, but hearing him say it at the table made me feel exposed. I said, "Yes, arranging is work. That is why it cannot all be mine." He said I knew the systems and he did not. I reminded him about the one-page reference sheet. He said the senior transport line had put him on hold for twenty-six minutes the last time he tried.

THERAPIST: Was that new information?

AVERY: Completely. He had tried after Dad's eye appointment got moved. He never told me because he eventually drove him himself. I had that ride in the ledger as Daniel driving. I did not have the hold time or the rearranged shift.

THERAPIST: What happened when you learned that?

AVERY: I said, "I did not know that." Daniel said, "Right." Not triumphantly. More tired. Then Mia said, "This is what I mean when I say your list counts what reaches you." I wanted to defend the list. Instead I said, "That is fair." It felt like swallowing a thumbtack.

THERAPIST: Did acknowledging it change the conversation?

AVERY: For about thirty seconds. Daniel softened. He said he could own one ride or arrangement per month if we gave him at least a week's notice, and he could be first call for house issues. I said house issues did not solve transportation. He snapped, "Nothing counts unless it is the thing you picked."

THERAPIST: What did you say?

AVERY: [faster, contained anger] "Because the things I picked are the things waking me up." He said, "And the furnace does not count because it wakes me up." I said I had not said repairs did not count. He said my spreadsheet had.

THERAPIST: Did he see it?

AVERY: Not then. He knew from Mia that repairs were in notes. Apparently Leah was not the only person who noticed the hierarchy. Mia and I had talked about it after I told her I was not bringing it out. She told Daniel. That felt like a betrayal even though it was not confidential.

THERAPIST: Did you name that feeling?

AVERY: No. I said, "Can we not argue about formatting?" which was dishonest because I was furious about formatting.

THERAPIST: What would you have said if you had named it directly?

AVERY: "I told Mia something while trying to prepare, and I feel exposed that it became evidence in the room." But that would have made Mia responsible for protecting the spreadsheet's reputation. I had not asked her to keep it private. She was talking to her brother about a meeting they were both attending.

THERAPIST: So what did you do with the anger?

AVERY: I put a fingernail into the bent index card. Later there was a little crescent torn out of the edge. Dad noticed and slid a napkin toward me like I had spilled something. That nearly made me cry. Not because it was profound. Because it was such a Dad gesture—solve the visible problem with the object nearest to him.

THERAPIST: Did the transportation discussion reach an agreement then?

AVERY: Not then. We parked it. I hate parking topics because they rarely come back, so I made everyone agree we would return after mail. Daniel said, "You can put a star on your card." I did not. Mia put a sugar packet on top of the card as the marker. It was childish and surprisingly effective.

THERAPIST: Were there lighter moments alongside the conflict?

AVERY: Yes. Dad complained that none of us had poured him more coffee while discussing his autonomy. Daniel saluted and poured it. Mia said that was not an approved care task. We laughed. The laughter did not erase the argument; it gave our bodies somewhere else to go for ten seconds.

## Part 3
THERAPIST: [attentive, inviting sequence and ambiguity] Take me through the point where the meeting nearly stopped.

AVERY: We moved to mail and bills. I proposed Mia sit with Dad twice a month to sort envelopes, and I would keep the scheduled online bill window because Dad wanted me to retain access. Mia said she could sort mail once a month but not twice during the school term. Dad said he did not want anyone sorting his mail.

THERAPIST: How did he say it?

AVERY: Calmly. "My mail is my mail." Then he pulled a stack from the sideboard to prove he knew where it was, and an overdue utility notice fell out. Not a shutoff notice. A reminder. The timing was brutal.

THERAPIST: What happened in the room?

AVERY: Silence. Dad picked it up and said, "I was going to pay this." I said, "It was due last week." He said, "Then I am a week late." Mia touched his arm. Daniel looked at me in a way that said do not pounce. I pounced.

THERAPIST: What did that look like?

AVERY: I said, "This is why a scheduled review is not an insult. We cannot discover overdue bills by accident." Dad said, "We? It is my bill." I said, "It becomes we when you call me to fix the late fee." He said, "I paid your bills when you were twenty." I said, "That is not the same thing." Then he said, "No, because I did not make a meeting about it."

[SILENCE - 7 SECONDS - DO NOT SPEAK]

THERAPIST: What is happening as you remember that?

AVERY: [softer, hurt held under precision] My chest is tight. I was humiliated. Not because he helped me when I was twenty; he did. Because he turned care into a debt I owed him, and everyone went quiet.

THERAPIST: Did you respond at the table?

AVERY: I said, "If this is repayment, there is no amount that finishes it." My voice was very calm. Leah is right that calm can be the warning. Dad said, "I did not say repayment." I said, "You brought up my bills to tell me I have no standing to object." Mia said, "Avery, stop telling him what he means." I told her not to police my reaction.

THERAPIST: And Daniel?

AVERY: He said, "We are doing the old scorecard now." I said, "I did not bring up the scorecard." He said, "You brought a spreadsheet to Dad's house." I said it was in my bag. He said that was not the defense I thought it was.

THERAPIST: Did anyone raise their voice?

AVERY: I did. Not screaming. Loud enough that Dad leaned back. Mia said my name sharply. I stood up because I felt trapped in the chair. Daniel stood too, which made everything feel more charged. He was not threatening me; I do not want that inferred. He said he was getting water. I said I needed a minute and went to the kitchen.

THERAPIST: Were you able to take the minute?

AVERY: Four minutes. I stood at the sink looking at the same chipped mug I used as a child. Dad and Mia were talking quietly. Daniel came in, kept distance, and filled two glasses. He said, "I am not saying you have not done more." I said, "Then please stop making the document the whole issue." He said, "Stop making the document the only reality."

THERAPIST: What did you hear in that?

AVERY: At first, attack. Then something like a request to be visible too.

THERAPIST: Did you say that?

AVERY: I said, "I did not know about the transport call." He said, "There are things you do not know." I wanted to ask for a list, which would have ruined the moment. I said, "I believe you." I was not fully sure I did, but I wanted to.

THERAPIST: What happened during the rest of the kitchen conversation?

AVERY: Daniel said he avoids telling me about effort because I ask follow-up questions until the story becomes a procedure. I said follow-up questions are how I understand. He said, "Sometimes I just want you to know I tried." I asked whether he wanted credit. That was a mean question. He said, "Yes, actually. The same thing you want."

THERAPIST: How did you respond?

AVERY: I looked at the water glass. I said, "That is fair, and I do not know how to give credit without pretending the gap is covered." He said, "Try saying both sentences." It was irritatingly close to what we practice here.

THERAPIST: Did either of you apologize?

AVERY: I apologized for the word credit. He apologized for saying nothing counts. He clarified that many things count but some things count more in my system. I said that was true because some tasks create immediate consequences. He said repairs also do. We did not resolve the hierarchy. We did return to the table with water.

## Part 4
THERAPIST: [slower, direct, comfortable with emotion] What happened when you returned to the table?

AVERY: Dad apologized first, which made it worse before it made it better. He said, "I should not have brought up when you were twenty." Then he said, "But I need you to stop talking as if I am gone." I sat down.

THERAPIST: What did he mean by gone?

AVERY: He said we discussed his calls, rides, mail, and appointments as if he were a route to manage. He said, "Nobody asked what days I want people here." Mia started crying quietly. Daniel stared at his hands. I felt defensive because I had opened with his decisions. I had said the correct sentence.

THERAPIST: But he was describing how the meeting felt to him.

AVERY: Yes. I said, "What days do you want people there?" He said that question was still about scheduling him. Then he laughed at himself and said, "I do not know how to ask this right." There was a long pause.

[SILENCE - 13 SECONDS - DO NOT SPEAK]

AVERY: [gentler, uncertain] Mia asked whether he wanted more visits that were not errands. He said yes, but not because he was lonely, which of course meant he was lonely. Daniel asked if Sunday baseball counted. Dad said only if Daniel stopped checking the basement during the seventh inning. They both laughed.

THERAPIST: And you?

AVERY: Dad looked at me and said, "Sometimes I want you to come without your bag." I knew he meant the folder bag. I said, "Sometimes I do not know how." That was the first honest sentence I said without planning it.

THERAPIST: What followed?

AVERY: He said, "We can learn." Very Dad. Simple, optimistic, slightly infuriating. I said learning still required the bills to be paid. He said yes. We were both trying not to let the emotional moment erase the practical problem.

THERAPIST: That sounds important.

AVERY: It was. Mia proposed she visit Sunday evenings twice a month with no assigned errand, and once a month she and Dad would open mail only if he agreed that day. Dad said yes to trying. Daniel offered baseball Sundays and one ride a month with a week's notice. I said one ride did not cover the gap.

THERAPIST: How did you say it?

AVERY: Too fast. "That still leaves me with at least two." Daniel said, "Can you receive one thing before asking for the next?" I told him the calendar would not pause for gratitude. Mia said, "This is the edge, Avery. We say yes and you immediately show us why it is not enough."

THERAPIST: Did you agree?

AVERY: [defensive, then correcting herself] I said, "Because it is not enough." Then I heard the totality in it. I corrected: "It helps and it does not cover everything." Daniel said that was different. I thanked him for one ride. It sounded forced because it was partly forced.

THERAPIST: Was it also true?

AVERY: Yes. One ride I do not arrange is one ride. The conflict is that a real contribution can still leave an unfair distribution. My family wants appreciation without the second clause. I want the second clause without pausing for appreciation.

THERAPIST: Did the transportation topic come back?

AVERY: The sugar packet worked. Daniel offered one ride he would claim from the monthly list and one additional ride only if his schedule allowed. Mia said she could take one school-break appointment but not promise monthly. Dad said he could use the senior shuttle for visits that did not require one of us in the exam room, if we helped him register once.

THERAPIST: Had the shuttle been tried before?

AVERY: No. We had all discussed it as if it were either salvation or abandonment. Dad said he wanted to try one short route before deciding. Daniel offered to register with him. I nearly took out my phone to find the form. I asked Daniel whether he wanted the link instead. He said he already had it from the reference sheet.

THERAPIST: What did you notice?

AVERY: Embarrassment and relief. Information had transferred even though nobody used my preferred system. That is apparently possible.

THERAPIST: What remained uncovered?

AVERY: At least one or two rides in a busy month. We agreed to name those when the calendar is posted rather than pretend we had solved them. That uncertainty is real. The meeting did not produce infinite capacity.

## Part 5
THERAPIST: [quiet, tentative, allowing disagreement] I am noticing that you held a limit in some moments and overfunctioned in others.

AVERY: [immediate irritation] That sounds like grading.

THERAPIST: Say more.

AVERY: I just gave you a complicated meeting, and you divided it into successful boundary and failed boundary. That is the same table with different vocabulary.

THERAPIST: I can see that. I compressed it too quickly.

AVERY: You did. I am tired of every action becoming evidence of whether I am recovering correctly from caregiving.

[SILENCE - 9 SECONDS - DO NOT SPEAK]

THERAPIST: Let me try again. Where did you have choice, where did you feel pulled, and what remains unclear?

AVERY: [guarded, slowly returning] Better. I had choice when I left the spreadsheet in the bag. I felt pulled when the overdue notice fell out. I had choice when I went to the kitchen instead of continuing louder. I felt pulled when Daniel offered one ride and I heard the remaining two before I heard the one.

THERAPIST: And what remains unclear?

AVERY: Whether the agreement is real. Everyone says things at family meetings. The test is Thursday at five when Mia has a school event, Daniel is working, and the pharmacy closes at seven.

THERAPIST: What did the group decide for that scenario?

AVERY: Mia flags by Wednesday. Daniel answers by evening. If neither can drive, they check delivery. If delivery fails, the group names the gap. I decide whether I can cover, but it is not assumed. We actually wrote that last line together.

THERAPIST: Who wrote it?

AVERY: Dad. On the back of an envelope, which was almost too symbolic to tolerate. He wrote, "Ask Avery; do not assign Avery." His handwriting is shakier than it used to be. I had to look away.

THERAPIST: Did anyone notice you looking away?

AVERY: Mia did. She did not touch me or announce it. Later, while Daniel and Dad were looking at dates, she asked quietly whether I wanted to step outside. I said no. She said okay. That was care in her style: an offer without insisting I display the feeling.

THERAPIST: How was it to receive that?

AVERY: I almost missed it because it did not solve anything. Then I realized that is the point. Not every supportive act needs to reduce the task list. I thanked her in the car afterward by text. She replied with a heart and then a correction to the Sunday schedule, which is the most Mia response possible.

THERAPIST: Did your interaction with her repair the earlier tension about the spreadsheet?

AVERY: Partly. I said I felt exposed that she told Daniel about the notes column. She said she was sorry she had not warned me but not sorry they discussed how the document represented their work. I could accept that distinction. I told her the document was private but not secret. We did not hug. We passed the coffee cake.

THERAPIST: What came up?

AVERY: Grief. Love. Annoyance that he could summarize in five words what took me seven sessions. Fear that he will forget the sentence. Fear that I will use his forgetting against him.

THERAPIST: What would using it against him look like?

AVERY: Holding up the envelope like a contract: "You agreed." He may not remember every detail. We need reminders without courtroom energy.

THERAPIST: How might you remind him?

AVERY: "Remember, we are trying the group first for non-urgent tasks." Not, "We had a meeting and you promised." If it is urgent, I want him to call whoever he can reach. We did define urgent examples: new severe symptoms, a fall, immediate medication concern, anything the clinic told him not to wait on. We are not making medical judgments ourselves.

THERAPIST: Was there any current safety concern during or after the meeting?

AVERY: No. Dad was medically stable that day. The conflict was verbal. Everyone drove safely. I was upset, not unsafe. I called Leah before leaving but sat in the car until my hands stopped shaking.

THERAPIST: What did Leah say?

AVERY: "Do you want listening, food, or analysis?" I said food. She said she would start rice. She did not ask for the result until I got home.

## Part 6
THERAPIST: [collaborative, precise, non-celebratory] How did the meeting address money?

AVERY: Badly and then somewhat honestly. The ledger included out-of-pocket costs. I had paid for delivery fees, parking, and two copays Dad reimbursed later. Mia had groceries she never submitted. Daniel had bought hardware and a space heater. Dad became embarrassed and said he could pay his own way.

THERAPIST: What was the actual concern?

AVERY: Not insolvency. Visibility and timing. Dad can cover routine costs, but reimbursements happen late because receipts live in three places. I also wanted the cost of my paid leave acknowledged, which is not something Dad can simply reimburse without turning family care into wages.

THERAPIST: Did you say that?

AVERY: I said, "Money is not only receipts. I have used six partial leave days." Daniel said he had traded shifts. Mia said she had used planning periods to make calls. Suddenly we were competing in sacrifice. Dad said, "I cannot afford all of you proving who loves me most."

THERAPIST: How did that land?

AVERY: Like a slap and a relief. I said it was not about proving love. Mia said, "It starts sounding like it." Then she told us she had not submitted grocery receipts because Dad always tried to pay her immediately and it made the visit feel transactional. Daniel said he needed reimbursement because he could not float hardware. Different needs, same family.

THERAPIST: What agreement emerged?

AVERY: A shared expense note with receipts, reviewed by Dad and me during the scheduled bill window. People can mark "reimburse," "gift," or "discuss." No commentary about whether someone should have spent it. For larger purchases, ask Dad first unless it is a genuine immediate safety issue.

THERAPIST: Did you create the note?

AVERY: [sheepish, restrained] At the table. On my phone. This may be the overfunctioning you were reaching for earlier.

THERAPIST: Do you experience it that way?

AVERY: Partly. Everyone was talking at once, and I made the document because I know how. But I assigned Daniel as co-owner before sending it. He rolled his eyes and accepted. He changed the title from "Dad expenses" to "Things we bought because life costs money."

THERAPIST: Did you change it back?

AVERY: I hovered over edit for a full minute. Then I left it. The title is terrible. It is also not harmful. That may be my greatest clinical achievement.

THERAPIST: [light dry humor, still grounded] I will refrain from issuing a certificate.

AVERY: Thank you.

THERAPIST: What did you do after you got home?

AVERY: I wanted to send a formal summary immediately. Instead I ate with Leah. I gave her fifteen minutes of facts. Then we watched half an episode of something and I did not absorb any of it. At nine thirty I drafted the summary.

THERAPIST: How long was it?

AVERY: Four hundred and twelve words. Leah did not review it. I slept on it and cut it to six bullets in the morning. I sent it at ten fourteen. Mia added one correction about Sundays. Daniel reacted with a check mark. Dad called and said, "Looks official." I asked if that was a complaint. He said, "It is an observation."

THERAPIST: Did you correct anyone's method after sending it?

AVERY: Not yet. Daniel entered a ride in the shared calendar as "Dad somewhere medical," which makes my skin itch. The location and time are in the details. I have not renamed it.

THERAPIST: Have you checked whether he completed the shuttle registration task?

AVERY: Twice in my mind, zero times with him. The agreed checkpoint is Friday. Today is Tuesday. I opened the shared folder once and closed it before searching for confirmation. That is not serene trust. It is behavioral noninterference with a lot of internal commentary.

THERAPIST: What does the commentary say?

AVERY: "He will forget. The form will require insurance information. Dad will call you Friday afternoon. You could prevent all of this in seven minutes." Then another voice says, "Seven minutes times every task is your entire life." They are both persuasive.

THERAPIST: Anything else happen after the summary?

AVERY: Dad called Mia instead of me about a grocery substitution. She handled it and mentioned it in the group afterward. I felt left out and relieved, which is ridiculous because being left out was the assignment. I almost asked what brand they chose. I did not.

THERAPIST: Does not knowing the brand matter?

AVERY: Only to the part of me that equates knowledge with care. Dad had groceries. Mia knew. I did not need to complete the triangle.

## Part 7
THERAPIST: [concrete, calm, emphasizing ownership] What do you want to test before our next meeting?

AVERY: The agreements for two weeks without daily evaluation. I can note what happens, but not score it every night. Medication backup follows the new chain. Daniel arranges one ride. Mia and Dad try one no-errand Sunday and one mail sort if he agrees. The bill window is this Sunday.

THERAPIST: What does "note, not score" mean in practice?

AVERY: Facts only: task, owner, outcome, needed change. No red. No percentage. No column called emotional labor, because I would pretend it was measurable and make everyone furious.

THERAPIST: Where will resentment go if it cannot go in the sheet?

AVERY: Here. My journal. Leah, with consent and a time limit. Directly to the person if there is a request attached. Some of it may just remain resentment for a while. I do not like that, but documenting it does not metabolize it.

THERAPIST: What is the real conflict you expect next?

AVERY: Dad calling me during Mia's no-errand visit to ask about a bill. Or Daniel forgetting to confirm the ride. Or me checking the pharmacy app before Wednesday because uncertainty feels like negligence.

THERAPIST: What about conflict with Leah?

AVERY: She may get tired of every family experiment becoming the central weather of our house. Sunday I started explaining the expense note while she was chopping onions. She put down the knife and said, "I am glad there is a plan. I cannot review the database." I was offended because it is not a database. Then I realized that was exactly the wrong response.

THERAPIST: What did you do?

AVERY: I asked whether she wanted to hear one emotional thing rather than the structure. She said yes. I told her Dad had asked me to come without the bag. She cried for a second. I did too. Then we finished dinner. The expense note waited.

THERAPIST: What made that exchange different from Tuesday night with the spreadsheet?

AVERY: I heard her limit before turning it into rejection. She made room for one thing, not nothing. I chose the thing that mattered to me instead of demanding she absorb the whole apparatus.

THERAPIST: Which is yours to practice with?

AVERY: The app. I cannot control whether they follow through. I can choose not to preempt them before the agreed checkpoint.

THERAPIST: What if the checkpoint arrives and the task is uncovered?

AVERY: I name it in the group: "This is still uncovered. What option are we using?" I wait. If there is a real deadline, I include it. If I choose to cover, I say it is coverage for this instance, not silent ownership.

THERAPIST: What if you are too angry to write that neutrally?

AVERY: Draft it, wait ten minutes, remove the history paragraph. If still too angry, say, "I need someone else to coordinate this one." I am allowed to sound annoyed. The goal is not a customer-service voice.

THERAPIST: How do you want Jeremy's voice in your head at that point?

AVERY: [faint humor, more relaxed] I do not want Jeremy's voice in my head. No offense. I want my own voice without the courtroom cadence.

THERAPIST: None taken. What does your own voice sound like?

AVERY: Clear. Not sweet. Not icy. Maybe, "I cannot take this ride. Please reply by six if you can." Then no appendix.

THERAPIST: That sounds usable.

AVERY: It sounds risky because there is empty space after it.

THERAPIST: Do you want to practice the empty space?

AVERY: Apparently this is an exposure-based silence clinic now.

THERAPIST: Only if you choose it.

AVERY: [small laugh that fades into uncertainty] Fine.

[SILENCE - 15 SECONDS - DO NOT SPEAK]

AVERY: My shoulders dropped around second ten. Before that I was composing the appendix.

THERAPIST: What was in it?

AVERY: Their schedules, Dad's needs, why I cannot, why the request is reasonable, and a preemptive apology. The usual furniture.

## Part 8
THERAPIST: [concise, warm but restrained, no inspirational cadence] What should we carry forward from the meeting without declaring it fixed?

AVERY: We made actual agreements. They are partial. Daniel's one ride is help and does not solve transportation. Mia's visits and mail sorting count even when they are not my chosen task. Dad wants help and wants to remain a person whose preferences can inconvenience us. I left the spreadsheet in the bag, then built a smaller document at the table. Both facts belong.

THERAPIST: And the conflict with your father?

AVERY: [voice softening, grief present without breaking] He should not have used the story about my bills at twenty. I should not have spoken as if an overdue reminder erased his right to privacy. He apologized. I told him later that I was scared when I saw the notice. He said he was embarrassed. We did not make either feeling the final argument.

THERAPIST: How are you with him now?

AVERY: Tender and irritated. He called this morning to tell me the coffee cake container was at my house. It was on his counter. I did not drive over to settle the location. We laughed. Then he asked whether I had sent Daniel the transport number, and I said Daniel already has the reference sheet. I did not resend it.

THERAPIST: How did that feel?

AVERY: Like leaving a small object on the floor and trusting another adult to see it. Uncomfortable, not catastrophic.

THERAPIST: How are you with Leah?
