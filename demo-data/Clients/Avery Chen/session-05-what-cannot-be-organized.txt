# Avery Chen session 5 What cannot be organized
# Fictional synthetic dialogue for transcript and longitudinal-synthesis evaluation
# Target duration 40 to 50 minutes

## Part 1
THERAPIST: How was this week after the pickup trial started? You said something happened during a visit with your father.

AVERY: It was quiet. I went over on Tuesday because he said his kettle was making a sound and he wanted help. We made tea — he likes it weak — and we sat at the kitchen table. He began telling a small, ordinary story about a woman in the building who bakes bread, and halfway through the sentence he lost the thread. He laughed and said, "I forget the punchline, but I like the loaf." It was gentle; he was laughing at the gap instead of panicking. I felt, I don't know, an immediate sadness and then fear that it would get worse. My hands started toward the drawer where I keep his paperwork, and I began to sort.

THERAPIST: When you say your hands started toward the drawer, what happened next?

AVERY: I pulled out a shoebox of random receipts, service flyers, a stack of appointment cards. I started laying them out like I do when I'm making a new filing system — invoices together, medical bits together, then thrown papers that probably belonged with other things. Part of me was thinking, "This is useful," and part of me was thinking, "If I make a list now, there will be fewer moments like this in future." I did not, importantly, check the family thread while I was there, which is a thing I noticed later. I paused before that reflex.

THERAPIST: Not checking sounds like a small but deliberate pause.

AVERY: It was. A pause that felt oddly both brave and stupid. Brave because I didn't dive into verification; stupid because my brain kept running the ledger of what ifs. [pause]

THERAPIST: When he joked about the loaf, what did you do in that moment?

AVERY: I laughed, but it was a tight laugh. I told him the joke for him: "Maybe she sells the punchlines with the slices." He smiled and reached for his mug. Then I went back to the papers because the sorting felt like action.

## Part 2
THERAPIST: You said sorting felt like action. Tell me more about the difference you noticed between acting and responding to what you felt.

AVERY: Acting felt tidy. It was measurable: if I grouped receipts, there was a visible before and after. Responding to sadness felt ... messy. For instance, I could have stayed with Dad and asked more about his day, or I could have said, "That joke hit me." But I started a task instead. I tell myself it was practical — "these papers are useful later" — but I suspect it was a way to not sit with the feeling of him losing a story.

THERAPIST: What does it feel like in your body when you choose the papers instead of the feeling?

AVERY: A kind of nervous energy around my mouth and hands. My jaw tightens. My fingers go fussy. There's also a low-level relief, like I have done something that might be counted later. Then later, after the sorting, a wave of tiredness that is more like grief than exhaustion.

AVERY: There was a moment when he looked at the neat pile and said, "You're good at this." He said it kindly, and I felt stung because it reminded me that my usefulness is a thing he values.

THERAPIST: He offered appreciation and you felt sting. How did you sit with that?

AVERY: Badly. I half-smiled and said, "Someone has to keep it from being chaos." Then I thought, I should have just said, "I like you." Instead I reorganized a prescription card.

THERAPIST: You noticed the instinct; that noticing itself is information.

AVERY: It is. It doesn't change the compulsion, but it starts a conversation. I felt like an archeologist of my own responses: cataloguing artifacts rather than the living moment.

## Part 3
THERAPIST: You mentioned earlier you didn't check the family thread while you were there. How did that choice feel in the moment and afterward?

AVERY: In the moment, it felt possible to leave the phone face down. My thumb hovered a couple of times — muscle memory — but I resisted. Afterwards, I had a small guilty pride: I hadn't intervened. But then later that evening my brain replayed what ifs: what if Mia had left the meds in the wrong place, what if Dad forgot them. The pause didn't erase the background noise of worry; it just didn't let me act on it immediately.

THERAPIST: What did you do with those what ifs?

AVERY: I started a list in the notes app titled "things to check tomorrow" and then deleted it. I alternated between telling myself the trial is working — Mia had taken the pickup — and rehearsing scenarios where the trial fails. There was no neat resolution, just that tug of wanting to be both present and practical.

THERAPIST: When you put the phone down at the table, did anyone notice?

AVERY: My father noticed that I was quiet. He said, "You're thinking, aren't you?" and I said, "Sort of," which is true and evasive. He asked, "About the meds?" and I said, "No, just about your loaf." He laughed and then asked about a neighbor's mail again. It felt like small connections threaded through the moment if I didn't interrupt them with my checklist.

THERAPIST: So not checking allowed different threads to exist.

AVERY: Yes. And also made those threads more fragile to me — because I know how quickly a thread can fray.

## Part 4
THERAPIST: Tell me about the joke he made — the one where he laughed at losing the punchline. What did that do for you and for him?

AVERY: He joked: "I forget the punchline, but I like the loaf." It was his quick pivot to keep the mood light. For him, it seemed like an adaptive move — a way to own the moment without making it heavy. For me, it was a double edge: relief that he wasn't distressed and a sudden grief that this was becoming part of our new normal. I smiled, but my throat tightened.

THERAPIST: You used "new normal." What does that phrase mean here for you?

AVERY: That things I didn't expect to manage — like the routine of forgetting — would become part of daily logistics sooner than I think is fair. I know that sounds dramatic. It is more like a steady accumulation of small changes: stories slipping, new notes on the fridge, different kinds of calls. Each one is small, but they add up into a pattern.

AVERY: When he laughed, I wanted to laugh with him and also freeze the moment so I could remember the texture of his breath and the steam from the cup. Instead I sort of did both — I laughed, and then I took a small pile of paper to the counter.

THERAPIST: You described wanting to remember sensory details. Did you at any point notice a ritual you might use to hold those moments without turning them into "things to fix"?

AVERY: I thought about taking a photo of his teacup, but that felt like documentation rather than presence. I thought about leaving a short paper note that said something like "liked the loaf" so we'd have a tangible snippet to read later. It felt less invasive than a photo and less administrative than a file.

THERAPIST: That sounded like the beginning of a low-demand ritual.

AVERY: Maybe. I'm tentative about rituals because I tend to make them into tasks.

## Part 5
THERAPIST: Let's follow that thread — the risk of making a ritual into another task. When you imagine leaving a small note, what do you worry it will become?

AVERY: A checklist item. "Leave note. Update notebook. File note under 'feelings'." I can see myself creating a binder of notes and then feeling bad if a week goes by without adding a new one. The ritual would calcify into another scoreboard. I know myself: rituals can become metrics if I allow them.

THERAPIST: And what do you want the ritual to be instead?

AVERY: A small, single act that can't be measured in productivity terms. Maybe a folded scrap with one line — "Liked the loaf" — left on the table. No tracking, no app integration. If I want to do it, I do it. If I miss a week, that's allowed. Low demand means it doesn't create more labor.

AVERY: Also I want it to be something Dad might notice and maybe smile at, instead of a formal update he has to read. The idea is to hold a memory, not to prove anything to anyone.

THERAPIST: That distinction — memory versus proof — matters to you. What would it feel like to succeed at that?

AVERY: A small ease in my chest. Less of the compulsion to tidy every ambiguous moment. Even if nothing changes clinically, it would feel like I'm carrying the moment differently.

THERAPIST: What would you call that ritual so it doesn't become an internal project?

AVERY: "Tea-note." Two syllables, no spreadsheet. If I label it too formally, I will turn it into a ledger.

AVERY: And it feels slightly ridiculous to have to name it like that, but naming is how I keep from overcomplicating.

## Part 6
THERAPIST: If you adopt "tea-note," how would you introduce it to your family or to Dad?

AVERY: I don't want to make it performative. With Dad, I'd just leave the note; no explanation needed. If someone asks, I'd say, "It's just a small thing." With the family, maybe I mention it in passing: "I might leave a little note when I'm over." The idea is not to create obligations for others.

THERAPIST: You worry about it becoming an obligation for you and for them.

AVERY: Yes. If I tell them and they expect it, then failing becomes a problem. So low demand means low announcement.

THERAPIST: You mentioned earlier that you began organizing papers after the visit. How did that organizing feel the day after?

AVERY: It felt like a hollow accomplishment. The pile was neater, I labeled a folder, and then I found an old appointment card with his handwriting and something in me just sat down and cried for five minutes. That was the grief I had been avoiding. Sorting opened room for the feeling, but it didn't fix anything. It was like scraping at a scab and finding something still tender underneath.

AVERY: Then I made a to-do note to scan the appointment card; immediately my brain tried to convert the grief into a follow-up item.

THERAPIST: You noticed the conversion. What did you do with that noticing?

AVERY: I put the phone away and called Leah. She let me say the words without turning them into tasks. It felt important.

THERAPIST: That sounds like the ritual you want: small, human, and not administrative.

AVERY: Yes. The phone away, the call with a person who doesn't file the emotion — that is a ritual I value.

## Part 7
THERAPIST: Returning to Mia's trial — you said earlier it was adequate, not perfect. How is that adequacy showing up in practical terms?

AVERY: Mia is consistent when she can be; she tells us what she did and where she put things. But her method differs from mine — a quick photo or a sentence — and I want different evidence. The trial is adequate because outcomes are being met, but it is not perfect because my anxiety asks for standardized confirmation.

THERAPIST: How have you managed the urge to standardize her method?

AVERY: I drafted a short message in my head many times and never sent it: "Could we agree on a confirmation format?" I paused before sending because I didn't want to impose my need as a rule. I did, however, mention to Leah that Mia's approach worked this week, which helped me register it as functioning rather than as an exception.

AVERY: I also tried the "no-check Thursday" rule we discussed. It worked two out of three weeks; the week it failed I opened the app for five minutes and then logged the urge. The log is laughably small: three lines, times and single-word triggers. It didn't erase the anxiety, but it gave me a record.

THERAPIST: The log gives information without immediate action.

AVERY: Right. And it's a curfew for my reflexive checking. I tell myself, "You don't have to fix every loose thread tonight."

THERAPIST: Is there anything in the family's response that surprised you?

AVERY: Daniel did step up once, which surprised me in the small good way. He covered a pickup when Mia had an exam. He didn't volunteer grand plans, just did a thing and texted, "Covered this one." That felt like a practical, quiet contribution.

AVERY: It's not perfect sharing, but it's functional. The surprise is that I felt both relieved and slightly mournful — relief because it worked; mournful because my role felt diminished even as fatigue lifted.

## Part 8
THERAPIST: You mentioned feelings of grief that overlay relief. Can you describe a moment this week where those feelings were particularly present?

AVERY: There was a quiet morning when I went to his apartment to bring a small lunch. He was seated at the table reading a flyer about a local concert. He smiled when I came in and showed me a flyer and started telling me about the performer. Mid-sentence he hesitated, looked at the flyer like it might help him find the thread, then he patted my hand and said, "You know what I don't need to remember?" He expected me to guess and I couldn't. The grief came as an immediate physical weight. I folded the napkins and put them on the plate because folding felt manageable.

THERAPIST: Folding again as a management strategy.

AVERY: Habit. I fold napkins to make the world look right. The folding calmed some of the chest pressure. Then he asked me whether I could look at the heater filter for him next week. I said, "I'll check who can help," which is deliberately non-committal. Internally I felt pulled between "yes, do it" and "no, do not take that on."

THERAPIST: You said you planned to choose one low-demand ritual of connection earlier. Did you find yourself wanting to make that ritual part of these moments — like leaving a folded napkin note rather than folding a napkin as a task?

AVERY: Yes. I'm thinking the "tea-note" could be a folded napkin with one sentence. It would be tactile, domestic, and small. That way folding isn't purely an avoidance; it becomes a deliberate gesture.

THERAPIST: That reframes the action from avoidance to connection.

AVERY: That is the hope. It might still be weird, but it feels like a small way to be present without converting presence into paperwork.

## Part 9
THERAPIST: Let's talk about the practical boundaries you set for verification. How did the family respond to your "if pickup isn't done by Wednesday, we activate backup" idea?

AVERY: I proposed it in the family thread as a question rather than a demand. Mia replied, "That seems fair." Daniel added, "If I hear by Wednesday morning, I'll cover." It wasn't a perfect chorus of commitment, but it was concrete enough to feel like a plan. Dad didn't engage with the thread — he never does — but when I told him in person he said, "I like hearing that there's a plan." He gestured to his chest in a way that suggested the plan comforted him.

THERAPIST: How did that exchange land with you?

AVERY: Part of me felt lighter because there was a named fallback. Another part felt tense because now there's an expectation that someone will be ready. The tension is: I want backup to exist without me being the one who remembers to activate it. The family agreed to a Wednesday check-in that is primarily a logistics nod, not a request for me to manage it.

AVERY: I also sent Mia a brief private note: "If you can't make it this week, text Daniel by Wednesday noon?" She said yes. It's all very small, but it reduced a slice of my anticipatory workload.

THERAPIST: These small communications are ways to redistribute cognitive labor.

AVERY: Exactly. The ledger is trying to be shared. I'm not letting myself add clauses to the agreement, which is an achievement. I wanted to add seven contingencies and I stopped at one.

THERAPIST: Stopping at one is restraint.

AVERY: It's my new hobby.

## Part 10
THERAPIST: You mentioned feeling anger briefly at the idea of continued assumptions. Can you describe what sparks that anger?

AVERY: Mostly the automatic assumption that I'm available on short notice because I've been available historically. So when Dad asks, "Can you fix this on Sunday?" my immediate reaction is irritation because I hear the unspoken continuation: "Because you always have." It feels like a default expectation that erases the cost of my labor. The anger is brief but sharp — a heat behind the eyes.

THERAPIST: And what do you do with that heat?

AVERY: I usually try to translate it into a boundary. I say, "I can help coordinate, but I can't promise to be there personally on Sunday." That sentence is awkward to say, and sometimes I cave and offer an earlier or later time instead of holding the boundary. So the anger signals that my boundary might be needed, but it doesn't always make me able to enforce it.

AVERY: Sometimes I also get snappish in the voice, which I dislike. Leah calls it "defensive snarl." She says, "Use the script." The script helps; it is bland and lifeless, but it moves the conversation without guilt.

THERAPIST: Scripts can be practical scaffolding. What would the ideal script be for that Sunday repair ask?

AVERY: "I can't be there this Sunday. I can ask Daniel to check availability, or we can hire a handyman. Which do you prefer?" It offers options and doesn't default to me.

THERAPIST: That gives agency back to him and boundaries for you.

AVERY: Precisely. And it's one of the moments where I'm trying to trade being the fixer for being the coordinator. It's less dramatic, more administrative, and frankly less exhausting.

## Part 11
THERAPIST: You said earlier that organizing papers allowed grief to surface later. How did you manage the emotion once it came?

AVERY: I allowed myself ten minutes in the car afterward to cry. It felt ridiculous but necessary. Then I made a sandwich and called Leah. The crying didn't fix anything; it was just a release valve. Later that evening I wrote a short entry in a journal that says, "He forgets small things, and I am tired." No solutions. Just a sentence.

THERAPIST: You created small containers for feeling — ten minutes, a call, a sentence. How did that serve you?

AVERY: It prevented escalation into obsessive controlling behaviors that would have consumed the night. If I didn't have that container, I would have probably started the "what if" list and then emailed the family a long corrective message. Instead, I let the moment be a moment and then returned to the household tasks with less reactivity.

AVERY: The container approach felt childish and effective. It's like putting the feeling in a tiny box and labeling it "Later." Not ignoring it, just setting a time.

THERAPIST: Is there any guilt attached to setting aside grief for later?

AVERY: A little. I worry that delaying means I won't process it properly. But the alternative is being hijacked by the feeling in moments where action is needed. So I'm experimenting with scheduled grieving.

THERAPIST: Scheduled grieving — you name a process rather than letting it ambush you.

AVERY: Exactly. It makes it less dramatic. And some days the ten minutes turn into thirty; it's flexible. The point is not to avoid but to ration my emotional energy.

## Part 12
THERAPIST: You described wanting to hand off knowledge to Daniel to reduce the single-point failure of contact. Did you create the one-page instruction sheet you considered?

AVERY: I did a draft. It lists the pharmacy numbers, delivery options, Dad's insurance ID, and a short checklist for what to do when a pickup is delayed. I kept it intentionally plain. I shared it with Daniel and Mia privately and labeled it "reference" rather than "Avery's manual," which was me trying to avoid the narrative that I was the only one who knew how to do it.

THERAPIST: How did they react?

AVERY: Daniel said "Helpful" and saved the file. Mia said "Nice" and asked if I wanted it on Google Drive. I didn't move to enforce where it lived; I said, "If you want it there, go ahead," and let them decide. That felt scary because the impulse was to ensure they put it in a place I'll notice, but I resisted.

AVERY: I also laminated a tiny card and left it in Dad's kitchen drawer. For him it's helpful to have a single card with essential numbers. For me, it felt like practical care without making me the person who remembers everything.

THERAPIST: Leaving the card in his drawer is a way to support without being the ongoing manager.

AVERY: Right. It feels like setting up the scaffolding and stepping away instead of living on the scaffold.

THERAPIST: How does it feel to step away?

AVERY: Light and terrifying in equal measure. The lightness is surprising; the terror is manageable in small doses.

## Part 13
THERAPIST: Leah has been a steady support. How did her role show up this week beyond the call when you cried?

AVERY: She sat with me while I wrote a message I was tempted to send to the family. She read it and said, "Shorten it." She refused to rewrite it into a prosecutorial memo, which is what I usually ask for when I want a co-sign. She offered small phrases instead, like "Thanks, team" and then asked me to put the rest aside for a day. She also reminded me about the "eight-minute rule" for composing messages — if it takes longer than eight minutes, save and wait.

THERAPIST: That rule has been useful before. How did practicing it go this week?

AVERY: Not perfectly. There was that one evening when I spent fifteen minutes composing a text about meal schedules and then deleted it. The eight-minute rule would have saved me that energy use. Leah's presence is less about fixing and more about holding a boundary with me. She is a witness who refuses to be a weapon, which is helpful.

AVERY: She also suggested the tea-note idea but in a less organized way—just, "Leave him something small, do it for no reason other than you liked him in that moment." That phrasing removed my need to make it consequential.

THERAPIST: That phrasing — do it for no reason other than you liked him — shifts intention from control to affection.

AVERY: Precisely. It makes it about presence. It sounds simple and a little corny but it's the sort of corny thing that my brain immediately wants to standardize, so I try not to.

## Part 14
THERAPIST: You said earlier you felt grief for an old identity — the person who was indispensable. Can you describe what you mourn about that identity?

AVERY: The certainty. The old role had clarity. If Dad called, I either did it or I didn't. People knew what to expect from me and I felt needed in a straightforward way. Now the role is diffuse. If I step back, I'm not sure how my value will be visible. I mourn that uncomplicated usefulness because it gave a kind of belonging.

THERAPIST: And what do you gain by stepping back?

AVERY: I get energy. I get to consider other parts of my life that have been deferred. I get to choose when and how to help rather than being on-call mentally. The trade-off is ambiguity about my place and guilt.

AVERY: There's also a quieter grief: I miss the small interactions that defined us — the particular way he pronounces a name, the way he hums. Those are not emergencies, but they are losses that accumulate and are not easily cataloged under "tasks."

THERAPIST: Those losses are not logistical; they are relational and sensory.

AVERY: Yes. And writing about them doesn't lead to a checklist. It leads to a feeling. I'm trying to honor that without turning it into a remediation project.

THERAPIST: Which is exactly the tension we've been naming.

AVERY: It is. And sometimes I falter into doing both: I honor and then I make a file to keep honoring later.

## Part 15
THERAPIST: You described relapse signals for reclaiming control earlier. How did those signals appear this week?

AVERY: A few. I found myself rereading Mia's messages multiple times, composing a corrective note and saving it as a draft for hours, and feeling a rising tightness when Dad assumed I would do a repair. I set the timer for eight minutes when I started composing a family thread message. At five minutes, I hit delete and wrote down a one-line script instead. That small interruption worked.

THERAPIST: When you saved the draft and revisited it, what happened emotionally?

AVERY: Embarrassment and then resentment. Embarrassment because the message read like an essay of control; resentment because I felt compelled to justify my boundaries. Saving and deleting taught me that my first drafts are often not what I want to leave as a record.

AVERY: I drafted a response to Dad's heater request and then walked away to make tea. The physical break lowered the intensity; when I came back I could say, "I can coordinate, but I can't be there Sunday." The word "coordinate" is a buffer.

THERAPIST: "Coordinate" shifts you from implementer to organizer.

AVERY: Yes, and it feels less intimate but more sustainable. It also gives Daniel a role to fill without dramatic confrontation.

THERAPIST: Those are small shifts that add up.

AVERY: They do. I'm trying to accumulate small shifts rather than one big transformation.

## Part 16
THERAPIST: We talked about the one-month review and a short evaluation form earlier. Did you implement that?

AVERY: I sent a three-question check-in: "Did pickups happen on time? Was Dad informed? Any clinical questions that needed escalation?" I asked for yes/no answers and a one-line comment if something went wrong. The responses were sparse but useful: Mia said yes on timing, Dad said he felt informed when I read his text to him, and Daniel noted he could cover when asked. There was one note about a delayed refill in week two, which triggered the backup plan and we managed the delay.

THERAPIST: How did seeing that data feel?

AVERY: Less dramatic than I feared. It didn't read like a performance review. It read like an operational status. That small shift — turning emotion into data for the purpose of decision — felt like a tool rather than a weapon.

AVERY: Looking at the responses also normalized that "adequate" is okay. Adequate kept medication on schedule and kept my involvement limited. It wasn't perfect, but it was workable.

THERAPIST: You designed the review to be two-sided, with you evaluating yourself as well. How did you fare on your own item about not initiating verification?

AVERY: Mostly a pass, with one exception when I called the pharmacy because their portal showed a delay. I logged that call and the trigger. It wasn't ideal, but it was an appropriate intervention. The fact that I'm treating even my slip-ups as data reduced shame.

THERAPIST: Data, not indictment.

AVERY: Yes. It changes the tone of the work.

## Part 17
THERAPIST: You said earlier you wanted phrases to counter shame in the moment. Did you use any this week? Did they help?

AVERY: I used "This is information, not indictment" a few times. It felt clunky out loud but it was useful. I also told myself, "Small repair beats spectacular collapse." That line is a little absurd but it stopped me from inventing dramatic consequences in my head.

THERAPIST: What did "small repair" look like this week?

AVERY: Apologizing for a curt text and rewriting it into a one-line clarification. Calling Dad to check a detail instead of the family thread and then asking out loud, "Would you like me to arrange someone for this?" Small, concrete moves rather than sweeping fixes.

AVERY: I also scheduled the Saturday walk reward and actually went without my phone. That felt like a small repair for my system.

THERAPIST: The walk was a chosen reward. How did it feel in terms of your nervous system?

AVERY: Surprisingly restorative. The air was colder than I expected; my muscles loosened. Not a cure — my chest still gave me a companionable ache when I thought about my father — but it made room. It felt like a small, deliberate kindness toward myself.

THERAPIST: Small kindnesses can be stabilizing.

AVERY: They are. I used to think only grand gestures counted. Turns out a walk and a nap also make a difference.

## Part 18
THERAPIST: You have spent a lot of time keeping details straight. I want to ask about uncertainty: what permission do you want in this moment to feel without fixing?

AVERY: Permission to be messy about my feelings. To notice the grief and not immediately catalog it. To say, "I'm sad today," without following up with "so I'll reorganize his pill drawer." To be tired and not explain it as productivity deficit. I want permission to have ordinary, unproductive sorrow.

THERAPIST: Would you give yourself that permission now?

AVERY: "You may be sad and still be caring. You may rest and still be responsible. You may miss the old role and still let it go a bit." Saying it out loud makes me feel slightly ridiculous and also a little better.

THERAPIST: How will you practice that permission between now and next session?

AVERY: Two things: the tea-note — leave one folded napkin note when I'm with Dad, not every time, just sometimes. And one ten-minute unplanned window each day where I sit with a memory without turning it into a task. No phones.

AVERY: Simple, low-demand. The point is to build tolerance for feeling without immediate resolution.

THERAPIST: Small practices to carry grief differently.

AVERY: Yes. It's not that grief disappears; it just doesn't have to be a to-do list.

## Part 20
THERAPIST: Let's rehearse the opening of the call you plan with Mia about pickups and also mention the tea-note if you want. How would you start?

AVERY: I'd start: "Is now a good time? I have a short request about one recurring task for Dad. Would you be willing to own medication pickups on Thursdays when refills are available and tell us by Wednesday if you can't?" Then I'll pause and listen. THERAPIST: That invites collaboration and a clear yes-or-no without pressure.

AVERY: Yes. If she says yes, I'll follow with, "Thanks. I will leave a little 'tea-note' on weekends sometimes — just a small folded napkin — so Dad has a small, silly memory. No obligation on anyone." If she seems puzzled, I'll say, "It's tiny and not a report — just a personal thing." Then I'll stop talking.

THERAPIST: And after the call?

AVERY: I'll write down exactly what she said and my exact wording, no interpretation, and then tell Leah the result. If I feel the urge to edit or control, I'll log it and take the Saturday walk reward if I make it through the week.

THERAPIST: That sounds like a concise plan that preserves your boundaries and the tea-note intention.

AVERY: It does. Nervous but okay. I don't feel like I've solved anything big, but I have a path to try.

THERAPIST: We will check in next week about how the call went and how the tea-note sits with you.

AVERY: Okay. See you next week.
