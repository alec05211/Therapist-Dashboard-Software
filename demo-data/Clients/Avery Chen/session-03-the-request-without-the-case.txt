# Avery Chen session 3 The request without the case
# Fictional synthetic dialogue for transcript and longitudinal-synthesis evaluation
# Target duration 40 to 50 minutes

## Part 1

THERAPIST: You brought two drafts.

AVERY: Three, if we count the one I wrote at one in the morning and deleted. I printed the others because showing you my phone felt too intimate, which makes no sense after everything I have said here.

THERAPIST: How did the apology go first?

AVERY: Better than I expected and not satisfyingly. I sent it after lunch like we discussed. Mia replied, "Thank you. I love you. I need a little space from logistics tonight." No thumbs-up.

THERAPIST: What was it like to receive that?

AVERY: Relief, then panic about the space. I wanted to clarify that I was not abandoning the plan. I did not. The next afternoon she called me.

THERAPIST: What did she say?

AVERY: She appreciated the apology. She also said she is willing to help but will not agree to a system where every delay becomes evidence that she should lose the task. I said that was fair. My voice sounded like a person signing a treaty under observation.

[pause]

THERAPIST: Did you believe it was fair?

AVERY: Yes. I also wanted her to acknowledge why I monitor. She did, actually. She said, "I know you have been holding too much." Then I almost turned that acknowledgment into permission to explain everything.

THERAPIST: What stopped you?

AVERY: I asked whether we could talk about one specific task after I had thought it through. She said yes, as long as it was not during school pickup.

THERAPIST: So today is preparation rather than persuasion after the fact.

AVERY: Exactly. I have not sent the medication request. I deserve a small medal for restraint.

THERAPIST: Or we can notice the restraint without turning it into another performance standard.

AVERY: Fine. A small unengraved medal.

## Part 2

THERAPIST: Which draft do you want to read first?

AVERY: The long one. It is embarrassing, but it contains the truth I am afraid the short one erases.

THERAPIST: Go ahead.

AVERY: "Mia, as you know, I have managed Dad's prescriptions, appointments, insurance calls, grocery problems, and most urgent questions since his health changed. I have done this while working full time and trying not to make the rest of you feel criticized. The pharmacy has made multiple errors, including the March refill delay and the incorrect automated message in June. Since your school is twelve minutes from Oak Street Pharmacy and you are usually nearby on Thursdays, I need you to take full responsibility for checking, collecting, and delivering Dad's routine medication refills every Thursday. This would include confirming the medication, communicating with Dad, and arranging coverage if you cannot go. I cannot continue to be the default person for every step."

[long pause]

THERAPIST: What happens as you hear it aloud?

AVERY: It is a closing argument. Also, "trying not to make the rest of you feel criticized" is objectively critical.

THERAPIST: What truths are in it?

AVERY: I have managed too much. The pharmacy has made errors. The task is more than physically walking into a store. I need ownership, not a favor that I coordinate.

THERAPIST: What is the draft trying to guarantee?

AVERY: That she cannot say no without appearing unreasonable. That she understands the full burden before answering. That if something goes wrong later, I can prove I warned her.

THERAPIST: Can a request guarantee those things?

AVERY: No. But documentation makes uncertainty feel less naked.

## Part 3

THERAPIST: Read the short version.

AVERY: "Could you own Dad's routine Thursday medication pickup starting next week? The Oak Street pharmacy closes at seven. If you cannot make a Thursday, please tell the family by Wednesday so Daniel or delivery can cover it. It is okay to say the recurring schedule does not work for you."

THERAPIST: What do you like about it?

AVERY: It is answerable. It names ownership. It has a backup that is not automatically me.

THERAPIST: What do you distrust about it?

AVERY: It sounds as if I am casually distributing chores from an equal baseline. It does not say I have carried this for a year. It does not say medication matters. It may sound cold.

THERAPIST: Does the request need the history to be legitimate?

AVERY: No. But I need the history to be witnessed somewhere.

[long pause]

THERAPIST: Is it witnessed here?

AVERY: Yes. That helps more than I want it to. Leah knows too. Mia knows pieces. Dad knows pieces and avoids adding them up.

THERAPIST: Could the family have a separate conversation about the overall imbalance?

AVERY: Eventually. If every task request waits for the family to agree on the history, nothing changes. We will spend three hours debating who drove Dad in 2019.

THERAPIST: What would you add to the short draft because Mia needs it, not because the case needs it?

AVERY: A sentence about what routine means. This is pickup and delivery, not making medication decisions. If the label or pharmacy instruction differs, she should ask the pharmacist and tell Dad to contact the prescriber. I do not want to transfer clinical judgment that none of us should be making.

THERAPIST: That sounds like scope rather than evidence.

AVERY: Yes. Scope is not a disguised accusation.

## Part 4

THERAPIST: What is the third draft?

AVERY: The friendly version. "Hey, would you maybe be able to help with Dad's meds sometimes on Thursdays since you are already over there? No worries if not."

THERAPIST: How does that compare with what you want?

AVERY: It is useless. "Help," "sometimes," "maybe," and "no worries" are four exits in one sentence. Then if she says yes once, I will act as if she accepted a recurring job.

THERAPIST: Why did you write it?

AVERY: To avoid sounding controlling. Also to avoid receiving a clear no.

THERAPIST: Ambiguity protects you from rejection.

AVERY: And guarantees resentment. I ask softly, interpret generously, then keep score privately.

[pause]

THERAPIST: Which draft is closest to respect?

AVERY: The short one. It treats her as capable of answering. The long one treats her as a jury. The friendly one treats her as if clarity would scare her away.

THERAPIST: What does respect for yourself require?

AVERY: Naming that I want recurring ownership, not occasional assistance. Not saying "no worries" when there would, in fact, be logistical consequences.

THERAPIST: Consequences are different from punishment.

AVERY: If she says no, we still need a plan. That does not mean I punish her or declare she does not care. It may mean asking Daniel, paying for delivery, or deciding I keep this task and transfer another one.

THERAPIST: Are all those options genuinely available?

AVERY: Delivery is available for most prescriptions, with some exceptions and timing issues. Daniel's schedule changes weekly. I am available, but I do not want my availability treated as infinite.

## Part 5

THERAPIST: Let us map the task carefully enough that the request is honest. How often are there pickups?

AVERY: Not literally every Thursday. Usually two or three in a month. The refill dates drift. Thursday became my day because I am near Dad's after work.

THERAPIST: Then "every Thursday" could imply a weekly trip even when nothing is ready.

AVERY: Good catch. I was about to overstate the burden in both directions. The ownership is checking the pharmacy message and handling pickup when a routine refill is ready that week.

THERAPIST: Who currently receives the message?

AVERY: Dad's phone and mine. That is a complication. We could add Mia as an authorized pickup person, but notifications may still go to Dad. He would need to forward the message or Mia would need portal access, and I do not know whether he wants that.

THERAPIST: His preference belongs in the plan.

AVERY: Yes. I almost designed the whole thing without asking him because asking him creates another variable.

[long pause]

THERAPIST: What do you know about what he wants?

AVERY: He is fine with any of us picking up medication. He does not like sharing passwords. He likes me knowing the medication list. That does not automatically mean he wants Mia to have portal access.

THERAPIST: Could the task work without it?

AVERY: Dad could forward the message to the family thread. Or the pharmacy may allow a pickup notification to a second number. I need to check without changing anything.

THERAPIST: Is checking that part of the request or preparation before asking?

AVERY: Preparation. But I can feel myself turning preparation into a six-week infrastructure project.

THERAPIST: What is the minimum information needed to ask whether she is open to the role?

AVERY: Whether Thursdays generally work and whether she is willing to handle the routine pickup when notified. Details can follow if she says yes and Dad agrees.

## Part 6

THERAPIST: What are you afraid will happen if you ask before solving every detail?

AVERY: She will find a flaw and use it as a reason not to do it. Or she will say yes, then ask me questions, and I will think I am still managing it.

THERAPIST: Is answering initial questions the same as retaining ownership?

AVERY: Not necessarily. A handoff has a transition. I resent that because nobody handed it to me cleanly.

THERAPIST: You learned under pressure.

AVERY: Yes. I want her to absorb the same difficulty as proof of fairness. That is not a good care plan.

[pause]

THERAPIST: What would a bounded transition look like?

AVERY: One conversation with Dad and Mia. A written note with the pharmacy address, hours, Dad's name and date of birth, and the rule that medication questions go to the pharmacist or prescriber. One practice pickup. Then I stop checking unless she asks or Dad reports a problem.

THERAPIST: How will you know ownership has transferred?

AVERY: Mia receives or is forwarded the readiness message, decides when to go before closing, communicates with Dad, and activates the backup if she cannot. I do not remind her Thursday afternoon.

THERAPIST: What if she forgets once?

AVERY: We look at the consequence and repair. If Dad has medication through the weekend, she picks it up Friday. If he will run out, the backup handles it. One miss is information, not immediate repossession.

THERAPIST: What threshold would make you reconsider?

AVERY: Repeated misses without communication. I do not know whether repeated means two or three. I do not want to legislate every possibility before she agrees.

THERAPIST: You can distinguish a backup plan from a surveillance plan.

AVERY: The backup tells us what happens when reality intrudes. Surveillance tries to prevent me from feeling uncertain.

## Part 7

THERAPIST: What happened with your father when you raised the possibility?

AVERY: I did not ask about Mia specifically. I asked how he feels about someone else handling pharmacy pickup. He said, "Anyone can stand in line." Then he asked whether I was too busy.

THERAPIST: What did you say?

AVERY: I said the family was trying to share routine tasks so no one person had all of them. He said, "You are the organized one." There it is again.

THERAPIST: How did you respond this time?

AVERY: I said organization does not create more hours. He looked hurt, though I may have been reading defensiveness as hurt.

[pause]

THERAPIST: Did he say what he felt?

AVERY: He said he did not ask me to do everything. I almost listed every time he had called me. Instead I said, "I know. We all fell into a pattern." He said Mia is busy.

THERAPIST: What did that mean to you?

AVERY: That my time is less protected. I said, "I am busy too." He said he knew. Then we sat there in the kitchen and heard the refrigerator make that alarming noise it makes.

THERAPIST: What happened next?

AVERY: He asked whether medication would cost more if Mia picked it up. Practical question. I said no. He said he did not want her bringing the grandchildren when someone at the pharmacy might be sick.

THERAPIST: A preference and concern you had not included.

AVERY: Yes. We could plan pickups when she is alone or use the drive-through. The point is, asking him produced information, not only resistance.

THERAPIST: Did he agree?

AVERY: He agreed to discuss it. Not the same as agreeing to the change. I am trying to keep those separate.

## Part 8

THERAPIST: How is Daniel involved?

AVERY: He replied to the family thread about backups. He can cover some Thursdays if he knows by Wednesday morning. His shifts are posted two weeks ahead, supposedly.

THERAPIST: Did you ask him directly?

AVERY: I wrote, "Could you be backup for pharmacy pickups when Mia cannot?" He wrote, "Depends on work." I almost sent a lecture about how all of us have work.

THERAPIST: What did you send?

AVERY: "Can you check your schedule and tell us by Wednesday morning when you are available?" He said yes.

THERAPIST: Did that answer the ownership question?

AVERY: Not fully. He is offering conditional backup, not taking the task. But conditional backup is still a role if the condition is clear.

[long pause]

THERAPIST: How does it feel to build a plan from partial capacities rather than equal shares?

AVERY: Unfair. Realistic. I want symmetry because it is easy to measure. Mia does Thursdays, Daniel does repairs, I do appointments. But tasks have different weight and people have different schedules.

THERAPIST: Does fairness require identical tasks?

AVERY: No. It requires the load not being invisible and one person's flexibility not becoming the family resource.

THERAPIST: How will you know if the new arrangement is fair enough?

AVERY: I have at least one recurring task I do not initiate, remind, or rescue. We revisit after a month. People can say what is working without me presenting a scorecard.

THERAPIST: Is medication pickup the best first task?

AVERY: It is concrete and bounded, but consequential enough that I will feel the handoff. That may make it a good test or a terrible one.

THERAPIST: What would make it safe enough to test?

AVERY: Dad's agreement, a clear notification path, a backup, and no changes to medication decisions.

## Part 9

THERAPIST: Let us rehearse the conversation rather than only the text. I can respond as Mia, but you should correct me if I turn her into a caricature.

AVERY: Please do not make her evasive. She is direct when she knows what is being asked.

THERAPIST: All right. Ask me.

AVERY: Mia, could you own Dad's routine medication pickups on Thursdays, starting next week? It would mean handling the pickup when a refill is ready, coordinating with Dad, and telling the family by Wednesday when a Thursday will not work so Daniel or delivery can cover.

THERAPIST: Thursdays are not always open. I have faculty meetings and Eli's soccer.

AVERY: I know, but I have been fitting this around my schedule for a year, and—

THERAPIST: What happened just then?

AVERY: I heard a no and opened the case.

[pause]

THERAPIST: Try responding to the information first.

AVERY: Which Thursdays are usually blocked? Would it work to own the task with the backup used on those weeks, or is the recurring responsibility itself not workable?

THERAPIST: I could probably do first and third Thursdays, but I do not want to promise every week.

AVERY: I am asking for ownership, not perfect attendance. If you know a week will not work, activate the backup by Wednesday.

THERAPIST: What if the prescription is not ready until Thursday afternoon?

AVERY: Then communicate when you know. The backup plan is not a moral test. We solve that week.

THERAPIST: That sounded different.

AVERY: I was speaking to a real constraint rather than proving mine is larger.

THERAPIST: How did your body feel?

AVERY: Tight, but not on fire. I could hear her answer.

## Part 10

THERAPIST: Let us try a harder response. "Why me? Daniel lives closer to Dad."

AVERY: Because the pharmacy is on your route and Daniel's shifts change.

THERAPIST: "Your route is near the pharmacy too."

AVERY: I am trying to transfer one recurring task because I currently hold most of them.

THERAPIST: "So this is about making things equal."

AVERY: It is about making the system sustainable. Equal may not be possible, but invisible is not working.

[pause]

THERAPIST: What do you notice?

AVERY: I want to list the fourteen tasks. But that last sentence may be enough.

THERAPIST: Would it be honest to say you are asking her because you need relief, not only because the route is efficient?

AVERY: Yes. "I am asking because I need one recurring responsibility to leave my list, and this seems compatible with your route. If it is not, I want a clear no and we can discuss a different task."

THERAPIST: How might Mia hear that?

AVERY: As direct. Maybe demanding. But not manipulative.

THERAPIST: Can a request include a need without making her responsible for all your exhaustion?

AVERY: That sentence does. The long draft hands her the entire accumulated burden and says fix it.

THERAPIST: What if she says, "I need to think"?

AVERY: Ask when she can answer. Maybe forty-eight hours. Not send additional evidence during the waiting period.

THERAPIST: What will waiting bring up?

AVERY: Every previous delay. I will want to interpret silence. I can tell Leah I am activated without asking Leah to predict Mia's answer.

## Part 11

THERAPIST: Try being Mia for a moment. What might make this request difficult for her beyond scheduling?

AVERY: She may feel I am assigning her a role because I think her work is less important. Teaching is treated like it ends at three. She has two children. Her husband travels some weeks.

THERAPIST: Anything relational?

AVERY: She may expect me to correct how she communicates with Dad. She may worry that saying yes once becomes permanent. She may be angry that I only notice her contributions when measuring them against mine.

THERAPIST: Does understanding those possibilities mean you should withdraw the request?

AVERY: No. It means I should not act surprised if she needs conditions.

[long pause]

THERAPIST: What conditions are you willing to accept?

AVERY: A one-month trial. Known exceptions for faculty meetings. Using delivery when available. A check-in after four weeks. Dad remains able to say he does not want the arrangement.

THERAPIST: What conditions would undermine the transfer?

AVERY: If I have to remind her every week. If "owning" means she waits for me to tell her the prescription is ready and asks me to arrange backup. If every exception comes back to me without discussion.

THERAPIST: Can you say that without assuming it will happen?

AVERY: "For this to leave my list, the notification and backup need to route to you rather than through me. I can help set it up once." That is clear.

THERAPIST: What if Mia proposes a different task instead?

AVERY: I should consider it. The goal is not specifically to make her stand at Oak Street Pharmacy. The goal is recurring ownership. But I may be attached to this task because I have rehearsed it.

THERAPIST: What other task could be equivalent?

AVERY: Weekly groceries, maybe. Or managing routine house maintenance calls. Those have different complexity. We would need to define them.

## Part 12

THERAPIST: You mentioned Leah. How has this process affected the two of you this week?

AVERY: Better after the apology. Leah said I seemed more present at dinner. Then I spent an hour formatting the long request, so let us not overstate progress.

THERAPIST: Did she read the drafts?

AVERY: No. I asked if she wanted to, and she said, "Only if you want feedback, not a co-author." I realized I wanted a co-signer.

THERAPIST: What would a co-signer provide?

AVERY: Proof my tone was acceptable. Someone else to blame if Mia reacted badly. Leah declined kindly.

THERAPIST: How did that feel?

AVERY: Lonely for a minute. Then respectful. She made tea and sat with me while I worked, but she did not edit.

[pause]

THERAPIST: Is that a form of support you can receive?

AVERY: I am learning. Presence without taking over. It is what I claim I want from my siblings too, though sometimes I want them to take over very specific things.

THERAPIST: Any protected time this week?

AVERY: Leah and I finished the terrible mystery. I put my phone on sound because there was no coverage plan, but I did not check it every ten minutes. Dad did not call.

THERAPIST: What was that like?

AVERY: Almost disappointing. I had prepared to demonstrate a new response and the test did not arrive.

THERAPIST: Rest did not need to become a performance.

AVERY: Apparently not. We ate popcorn. I missed half the plot because the plot was nonsense, not because I was thinking about medication.

## Part 13

THERAPIST: How are sleep and appetite?

AVERY: Sleep is uneven. Two decent nights, three bad. I ate lunch most days. The headaches are less constant.

THERAPIST: Any change in alcohol or other substances?

AVERY: No. One glass of wine Saturday with dinner. Nothing used for sleep.

THERAPIST: Any thoughts of harming yourself, not wanting to be alive, or harming someone else?

AVERY: No. I still want periods when no one can contact me. I do not want to disappear permanently. No thoughts of hurting anyone.

THERAPIST: Any concern that the family argument could become physically unsafe?

AVERY: No. Mia and I can be cutting, but there has been no threat or physical aggression. Dad is safe. Leah is safe.

THERAPIST: Thank you. What has anger looked like since last session?

AVERY: Lower volume. It appeared when Dad defended Mia's busyness. I noticed my polished voice. I said, "I am busy too," then stopped instead of presenting the calendar.

[long pause]

THERAPIST: What did stopping cost?

AVERY: I felt unseen. But I also heard his concern about the grandchildren at the pharmacy. If I had kept arguing, I would have missed that.

THERAPIST: Does listening mean conceding your time matters less?

AVERY: No. That is a useful distinction. I often act as if the only way to preserve my point is to keep speaking until everyone agrees.

THERAPIST: What can preserve it internally?

AVERY: Saying it clearly once. Writing it here. Making a decision based on it even if Dad does not validate it perfectly.

## Part 14

THERAPIST: Let us assemble the request you actually want to make.

AVERY: I want to call her, not text. Text makes me overedit and makes tone easy to invent.

THERAPIST: Start the call.

AVERY: "Is now still a good time to talk about one recurring task for Dad?"

THERAPIST: Good. Assume she says yes.

AVERY: "I need one routine responsibility to leave my list. Would you be willing to own Dad's medication pickups when refills are ready on Thursdays for a one-month trial? Ownership would mean coordinating with Dad, picking up before the pharmacy closes, and using Daniel or delivery as backup when you know Thursday will not work. I can help set up the notification once, but I would not remind you each week."

THERAPIST: Then?

AVERY: Stop talking.

[long pause]

THERAPIST: What is happening in the stop?

AVERY: She gets to think. I do not fill the space with the March pharmacy error or my work schedule.

THERAPIST: Is anything essential missing?

AVERY: Dad has agreed to discuss it, not fully agreed. I should not imply the plan is final. Maybe: "If you are open to it, the three of us would confirm the notification and pickup details before starting."

THERAPIST: That preserves his role.

AVERY: Yes. And if she says no, I ask whether a different recurring task is workable, but not in the same breath if she needs time.

THERAPIST: How long is the request now?

AVERY: About forty seconds. The long draft was three minutes before objections.

THERAPIST: Which one contains more room for Mia?

AVERY: The forty-second one.

## Part 15

THERAPIST: What does the long draft contain that still needs somewhere to go?

AVERY: My resentment that nobody noticed how many steps I was doing. Fear that one delegated task will be praised as equal contribution. Anger that I have to ask instead of people seeing. Grief that this is our family conversation now.

THERAPIST: Which of those might belong in a later family discussion?

AVERY: The invisible workload and how we review it. Maybe the resentment, if I can name it without using it as a weapon. Grief may belong in more than one place, including here.

THERAPIST: Which does Mia need before answering the task request?

AVERY: She needs to know I genuinely need relief and that recurring ownership matters. She does not need to absorb all my grief before she can say whether Thursdays work.

[pause]

THERAPIST: What is it like to separate being understood from getting an answer?

AVERY: Scary. If I am not fully understood, the answer may feel shallow. But demanding full understanding can be another way to control the answer.

THERAPIST: Does anyone ever fully understand the burden from the inside?

AVERY: Leah comes closest, and even she sees the outside. I do not fully understand Mia's week either.

THERAPIST: What would mutual partial understanding look like?

AVERY: Ask real questions. Believe the constraints she names unless evidence says otherwise. Expect her to believe me when I say the current load is unsustainable. Negotiate the task without deciding whose life is objectively harder.

THERAPIST: Can you hold that during the call?

AVERY: I can try. If I hear myself comparing calendars, I can say, "I am making this a case again. Let me return to the request."

## Part 16

THERAPIST: Before we settle on the call, I want to test what happens if Mia asks a question you did not prepare for. I say, "Why does this have to be ownership? Why can't you just text me when you need a pickup?"

AVERY: Because texting you when I need it means I still monitor the refill, decide when to ask, and remember whether you answered.

THERAPIST: I say, "That takes thirty seconds."

AVERY: The text takes thirty seconds. Holding the task in my head does not.

THERAPIST: Can you say that without proving the entire cognitive-load argument?

AVERY: "I am trying to transfer the remembering and coordination, not only the drive. If you only want occasional pickup requests, say that, but it would not meet the need I am asking about."

[pause]

THERAPIST: What do you notice?

AVERY: That is firm. I expect firmness to cause rupture, so I usually pad it until the meaning disappears.

THERAPIST: What if she says occasional help is all she can offer?

AVERY: Then I believe the answer. I can accept occasional help as occasional help without renaming it ownership. I still need to find another arrangement for the task.

THERAPIST: Does accepting the answer mean approving of the larger imbalance?

AVERY: No. It means planning with the capacity she states. I can still have feelings and a separate conversation about family responsibility.

THERAPIST: What would make you doubt the stated capacity?

AVERY: If she claims she can never do a recurring task but repeatedly commits to equally demanding optional things. Even then, I would need a conversation, not a private conviction about her character.

THERAPIST: The goal is not to make yourself unable to judge patterns.

AVERY: The goal is to judge them from patterns, not from the answer I feared before asking.

## Part 17

THERAPIST: How does your father's autonomy complicate ownership?

AVERY: He may change his mind week to week. He may pick up a prescription himself because he is already out. He may tell Mia not to come, then tell me nobody came. Not maliciously. He experiences plans as flexible suggestions.

THERAPIST: What would ownership mean if he remains the decision-maker?

AVERY: Mia coordinates with him rather than controlling him. If he chooses to pick it up, she confirms the task is complete and does not treat him as breaking a rule. If he declines her help, that is his choice unless there is a separate capacity or safety concern we have not established.

THERAPIST: Are there such concerns now?

AVERY: He forgets details sometimes and gets confused by automated messages. He still makes his own decisions. I do not want ordinary forgetfulness converted into a conclusion that we can override him.

[long pause]

THERAPIST: How will you include him without making the conversation about the burden he creates?

AVERY: Ask what notification method he is comfortable with. Ask whether he wants Mia to coordinate Thursday pickup. Explain that the goal is sharing family tasks, not monitoring his medication use.

THERAPIST: What if he says he only wants you?

AVERY: That would hurt. I would ask what specifically feels safer about me. Some concerns may be addressable. But his preference does not automatically require me to provide unlimited availability.

THERAPIST: Could you say that to him?

AVERY: "I hear that you prefer me. I cannot keep managing every part, so we need another person or delivery for this task." I would want to soften it.

THERAPIST: Is there warmth you can add without making the limit optional?

AVERY: "I am still involved in your care and I love you. Sharing this task is how I stay involved without burning out." That feels more honest than pretending it is only efficient.

## Part 18

THERAPIST: You have used the phrase burning out. What does it mean behaviorally for you right now?

AVERY: Sleep disruption, headaches, skipped meals, irritability, closing my office door, canceling Nora, treating Leah like another stakeholder. Not a diagnosis. A description of strain.

THERAPIST: What would improvement look like if the external demands remain real?

AVERY: Fewer tasks that only I can initiate. More predictable off periods. Eating lunch without deserving it first. Being able to hear Dad's stories without waiting for the assignment hidden inside them.

THERAPIST: Would one pharmacy task accomplish that?

AVERY: Not by itself. It is a test of whether the system can change. I need to be careful not to make it symbolize everything and then panic if the first week is messy.

[pause]

THERAPIST: How will you evaluate the one-month trial?

AVERY: Whether routine refills reach Dad on time. Whether Mia coordinates without weekly reminders. Whether exceptions activate a backup. Whether Dad feels informed and respected. Whether I actually stop checking.

THERAPIST: That last measure depends on you.

AVERY: Yes. Mia could perform perfectly and I could retain the task mentally. I need a rule for myself: do not open the pharmacy app on Thursdays unless Dad or Mia asks me to help with a specific problem.

THERAPIST: What if anxiety rises?

AVERY: Note it. Wait until the agreed check-in. Do something else that requires attention. If there is objective information that the pickup failed, respond to that.

THERAPIST: Could the evaluation become another ledger?

AVERY: Easily. I will record only exceptions and the end-of-month review, not minute-by-minute performance. No secret score.

THERAPIST: Will Mia know the review criteria?

AVERY: She should. A trial should not contain hidden standards.

## Part 19

THERAPIST: Let us imagine she says yes immediately. What is the risk then?

AVERY: I become enthusiastic and unload every detail. I send portal screenshots, the medication list, pharmacy history, Dad's insurance card, and six warnings she did not request.

THERAPIST: What does she actually need?

AVERY: Dad's consent. Pharmacy location and hours. Whatever identification the pharmacy requires. The refill notification path. The backup names. The boundary that questions about changes go to the pharmacist or prescriber.

THERAPIST: Does she need the complete medication list?

AVERY: Maybe not for pickup. The label and Dad's confirmation may be enough. Sharing more health information should be based on his permission and necessity, not my anxiety.

[long pause]

THERAPIST: What if the pharmacy gives her a different dosage than the previous label?

AVERY: She does not decide which is correct. She asks the pharmacist to clarify, and Dad contacts the prescriber if needed. She can notify the family that there is a delay. I do not want a family logistics plan to drift into medical decision-making.

THERAPIST: What if she calls you first anyway?

AVERY: I can redirect: "Please ask the pharmacist and Dad's prescriber; tell me what they say if Dad wants me informed." I do not have to reward the old route by taking over.

THERAPIST: Is there any circumstance where she should call you?

AVERY: If Dad asks for me, if the backup plan truly fails, or if there is contextual information only I have. But "Avery is faster" cannot remain the standing circumstance.

THERAPIST: How does it feel to define your own availability as a backup rather than the infrastructure?

AVERY: Risky. Also more accurate. I am still family. I am not removing myself; I am changing the order of operations.

## Part 20

THERAPIST: Imagine the call goes badly. Mia says, "This sounds like another system you designed without us."

AVERY: I would want to defend the design. Instead: "I drafted a proposal because I needed something concrete. What would make it collaborative rather than assigned?"

THERAPIST: I say, "Start by asking what I can do instead of telling me the task."

AVERY: "All right. Is there a recurring part of Dad's weekly care you are willing to own for a month? Medication pickup is the one I thought fit your route, but I am open to another bounded task."

THERAPIST: I say, "I need to see the whole list first."

AVERY: That is reasonable, though I would fear it becomes delay. I could share a short list, not the prosecution spreadsheet, and set a time to choose.

[pause]

THERAPIST: What if she says your definition of ownership is too rigid?

AVERY: Ask which part. Notification, pickup, communication, or backup. We can change the method while keeping responsibility visible.

THERAPIST: What if she accuses you of controlling?

AVERY: "I know I can overdefine tasks when I am anxious. I am trying to distinguish necessary scope from controlling the method. Tell me which detail feels like method rather than outcome."

THERAPIST: Would you be able to hear the answer?

AVERY: Not perfectly. I might need a break. I can say, "I want to think rather than argue. Can we return to this tomorrow?" A pause is not abandonment if we name when we return.

THERAPIST: What if she never returns?

AVERY: Then that becomes specific information. I follow up once at the agreed time. After that, I plan without her yes rather than chasing indefinitely.

## Part 21

THERAPIST: What would success in the conversation look like apart from the answer?

AVERY: I make one clear request. I do not use evidence to corner her. I hear constraints before evaluating them. I keep Dad's consent visible. I leave with an answer or a time for an answer.

THERAPIST: What would repair look like if you open the archive anyway?

AVERY: Stop. Say, "I am bringing in the whole history because I am afraid this request will not be taken seriously. Let me return to what I am asking now." I do not have to finish the argument because I started it.

[long pause]

THERAPIST: How is this different from suppressing anger?

AVERY: I am naming the fear and the history without making them compulsory reading for Mia. I can bring the anger here, to Leah with consent, or to a later family discussion. It still influences my boundary.

THERAPIST: What boundary?

AVERY: I will not remain default coordinator for every recurring task. If Mia cannot take this one, the family needs another named owner, delivery, paid support if feasible, or an explicit decision about which task leaves me instead.

THERAPIST: Does that depend on everyone agreeing your burden has been unfair?

AVERY: No. It depends on me being honest that the current arrangement is not sustainable.

THERAPIST: What emotion is here now?

AVERY: Sadness. Some relief. Anger is still present, but it is not driving the sentence.

THERAPIST: What are you grieving?

AVERY: That asking well does not guarantee help. That Dad needs more than he used to. That my siblings and I cannot return to being people who argue about holidays and recover with a funny picture.

THERAPIST: What remains possible?

AVERY: A different way of being family. Less automatic, more explicit. Imperfect. Maybe still funny sometimes.

## Part 22

THERAPIST: When will you call?

AVERY: Tomorrow at four-thirty. She said that is after dismissal and before soccer. I asked for fifteen minutes.

THERAPIST: What will you do before the call?

AVERY: Eat something. Read the forty-second version once. Put the long draft in a drawer, not beside me like backup ammunition.

THERAPIST: During the call?

AVERY: Ask if it is still a good time. Make the request. Stop. Ask questions I do not know the answer to. If she needs time, agree on when we will return to it.

THERAPIST: Afterward?

AVERY: Write down what she actually said before telling Leah what I think it means. Do not call Dad with a plan until Mia and I know whether there is one.

[long pause]

THERAPIST: What outcome would count as useful even if she says no?

AVERY: A clear answer. Information about which task she can own. Evidence that I can make a direct request without presenting a lifetime case.

THERAPIST: What remains outside your control?

AVERY: No. I need to stay clear enough to hear her.

THERAPIST: Anything else you want to take with you?

AVERY: Brevity is not erasure if I choose where the rest of the truth goes. A request can be specific without pretending the history is simple.

THERAPIST: And the safety check remains unchanged?

AVERY: See you next week.
