# Avery Chen session 6 The canceled plan
# Fictional synthetic dialogue for transcript and longitudinal-synthesis evaluation
# Target duration 40 to 50 minutes

## Part 1
THERAPIST: Tell me about Sunday — you mentioned tea and a song with your father and that there was no paperwork. What happened?

AVERY: I went over in the afternoon because he texted that he liked the playlist I made, and then he called — he said, "Come over, I heard that one song you like." We had weak tea, as usual, and he hummed through two lines, then asked the name of the singer. I sang along a little, and he smiled like he was following. There was no digging through drawers or checking his papers. It felt like one of the small mercies: ordinary, not urgent.

THERAPIST: You emphasize ordinary and not urgent. What did that feel like?

AVERY: Light. A kind of permission to be present without an agenda. I didn't go into the drawer. I didn't inventory the post-it notes. I sat, and when he asked, "Do you remember when..." I answered with whatever came. It felt fragile in a good way.

THERAPIST: Fragile in what sense?

AVERY: Like a glass you hold carefully because it's warm and you could drop it, not because it will shatter dramatically, but because it would change shape if you squeezed too hard. I didn't squeeze. I let it be soft.

THERAPIST: You chose not to squeeze. That was a deliberate choice.

AVERY: Yes. I had to decide to let the moment be what it was. The whole hour felt small and uncluttered, which was new in the middle of these weeks.

THERAPIST: You say "new." Does that surprise you?

AVERY: A little. I'm used to making a plan whenever I go over. Sunday felt like a break from that habit — intentionally, as if I was trying out a mode of visitation that didn't require me to fix or file afterward. It felt good in the moment and then oddly hard to put away when I left.

## Part 2
THERAPIST: You left feeling it was good and hard to put away. Can you say more about what "hard to put away" meant when you got home?

AVERY: At home, my phone buzzed with a Leah text confirming our dinner and bookstore plan for Wednesday evening. I felt buoyed and then my father's nurse called with a message about him feeling dizzy later Sunday night. I immediately phoned him. He said, "Just a bit lightheaded," and then he asked me to help call his clinic. I called as he asked; they suggested a few things and said they'd note it and call us back if they wanted a visit. My sense of the evening shifted from buoyant to precautionary. It was an ordinary disruption but still felt like a domino.

THERAPIST: You describe a shift. What did that feel like internally?

AVERY: A tightening that started low and moved up into my throat. I remember thinking, "Okay, logistics," then doing them. I called the clinic, left details, read the nurse the medications list he keeps. It was efficient and calm on the surface, but afterward I had a low-level hum of apprehension.

THERAPIST: You used "hum of apprehension." How did that affect the dinner plan with Leah?

AVERY: I texted Leah to say that his clinic had been notified and that we might need to reschedule if they recommended rest. She replied, "Let's wait to see." We both assumed we'd know by morning. When the clinic advised staying close and monitoring dizziness, I called Leah and canceled that evening. It felt like the right move, but later I told Leah there was no point rescheduling because something always happens. Saying that felt heavier than canceling itself.

THERAPIST: You said the sentence felt heavier. What came up when you said it?

AVERY: A resignation. A sense that planning was futile, like every plan eventually met an interference. That sentiment surprised me; it sounded like giving up before we even tried to set a new time.

## Part 3
THERAPIST: Let's slow to that sentence you told Leah: "there's no point rescheduling because something always happens." When you said that, were you naming a pattern or protecting yourself from disappointment?

AVERY: Maybe both. Part of me wanted to avoid the emotional work of making a plan that may not survive. Making a plan feels like putting hope on a shelf, and if I assume the shelf will collapse, then the disappointment won't be as fresh. The other part recognized there's a pattern — interruptions keep coming and it's exhausting to maintain optimism if it means perpetual reorganizing.

THERAPIST: You're holding both a protective move and an observation of pattern. How did Leah hear it?

AVERY: She said, "That's understandable," and then paused and added, "But I don't think we should stop trying." She sounded patient, not scolding. I remember feeling a small flash of irritation, partly because I had a sinking feeling I had echoed something to myself before I even checked how I felt about the loss of the plan. Later, I realized I might have told myself truth in the form of fatalism to avoid disappointment.

THERAPIST: You notice a defensive move that looks like truth to avoid disappointment. What did you do next?

AVERY: I told Leah I wasn't serious about giving up; it was more immediate tiredness than a philosophy. She suggested we tentatively pick another night and mark it as flexible. I said okay but also added, "But don't be surprised if something comes up." I said that partly as warning and partly to prepare myself in case the plan fell apart again.

THERAPIST: That phrasing functioned as both shield and forecast.

AVERY: Yes. It kept me from feeling guilty if I needed to cancel later, but it also seeded the outcome with resignation.

## Part 4
THERAPIST: When you offered the warning, did it change how you felt about rescheduling at all?

AVERY: It eased an immediate pressure but didn't remove that background sourness. Telling someone "don't be surprised" is a way to buffer disappointment, but it also reduces the energy I spend on the pleasure of anticipation. I think anticipation is partly what makes these plans matter. By pre-emptively guarding against disappointment, I diluted the possible joy before it had a chance.

THERAPIST: You used "diluted the possible joy." What happens in your body when you think of that dilution?

AVERY: I notice a flattening around my chest and a light heaviness behind my eyes. The imagined bookstore scene — walking in and recommending a book to Leah, then browsing — becomes less vivid. It feels greyed. I can still make it, but I don't get that little thrill I might otherwise. Instead there's a calculus: how much effort for how much chance of friction.

THERAPIST: That calculus replaces surprise with calculation.

AVERY: Yes, and it feels practical in one register and miserly in another. Practical because I can tolerate fewer disappointments; miserly because I skim off small joys to avoid potential pain. I don't want to live miserly.

THERAPIST: You said you don't want to live miserly. Is that a value you want to hold in decisions like these?

AVERY: I think so. I want to protect energy but also allow myself to be open to small pleasures. The trick is finding a middle path where I don't commit to heavy emotional investment but I also don't pre-emptively sabotage the potential enjoyment.

THERAPIST: That middle path — what might it look like practically?

AVERY: Maybe scheduling things as "tentative" with a soft deadline to confirm, so the plan exists but the emotional stake is reduced until it's closer.

## Part 5
THERAPIST: Tentative with a soft deadline. How did that idea land with Leah when you suggested shifting dinner to another day as tentative?

AVERY: She liked it. She said, "Let's aim for Friday and confirm by Thursday morning." That felt like a manageable frame. Leah's voice was steadier than mine; she treated the plan as a possibility, not a promise. That helped me imagine showing up without feeling trapped if it fell through.

THERAPIST: Leah's steadiness seemed to calibrate the plan for you.

AVERY: Exactly. I realized I was conflating "plan" with "obligation." Leah's version felt like an invitation with a clear accept-or-decline moment before it became a responsibility. I told her to pick a book to hold aside and she said she'd buy two copies if we both liked it, which made me laugh. It was small and practical, and it reintroduced a bit of the pleasure meter.

THERAPIST: You mention the pleasure meter. Does that meter have a reading now?

AVERY: Slightly higher than when I told her "there's no point." Maybe twenty percent of the excitement is back. The rest is guarded. I still have this thought that something will interrupt, but there's also curiosity about whether the interruption will actually happen. It's almost like I'm updating probabilities: before it felt like a 90 percent chance of interruption; now maybe it's 60 percent.

THERAPIST: Estimating probabilities sounds like a cognitive tool you use to manage expectations.

AVERY: I do that a lot. It feels useful, but it can also be a cover for pessimism. Running numbers reduces surprises, but it also keeps me from being surprised in a good way.

THERAPIST: Noting that tension seems important. When you revert to numbers, does it change how you behave?

AVERY: Yes. More checking, more contingencies. Less spontaneous pleasure. Less willingness to commit to a time that would allow real presence.

THERAPIST: And when you commit to tentative, with a confirmation point, you allow some spontaneity back in.

AVERY: Right. It's a small compromise that might restore some of the possibility.

## Part 6
THERAPIST: You said earlier that when your father reported dizziness you contacted his care team and followed their instructions. How did doing that work for you?

AVERY: It felt like a clear, finite task in an otherwise messy week. I read the list of medications to the nurse, noted their suggestions, and then documented the call in my notes app. I closed that loop and physically handed my father the carved sticky I keep for him with the clinic's number. On one hand, it was practical and calming; on the other hand, the cancellation of dinner followed and the old voice in me said, "See? This is why you can't plan."

THERAPIST: The action gave you a tidy, practical feeling, and the consequence was the canceled plan.

AVERY: Yes. The cancellation was the logical, responsible step given the instruction to monitor dizziness and avoid going out while it's new. I don't regret it. But afterward, I had to notice whether I was letting real risk dictate my whole social life or whether I was selectively letting safety become a reason to withdraw.

THERAPIST: You're distinguishing between legitimate caution and an overgeneralized withdrawal.

AVERY: Exactly. The clinic's advice was specific: monitor and keep him at home if dizzy. That justified canceling dinner that evening. What I worry about is my pattern of converting specific cautions into global rules. The belief that rest is futile extends beyond the clinical instruction and turns into a general defeatism about plans.

THERAPIST: How did you test that belief later with Leah or anyone else?

AVERY: I said to Leah, "Maybe we try again next week." She agreed, and I could feel my instinct to preface with the "something always happens" line. I stopped myself and instead said, "Let's tentatively pick Friday and confirm on Thursday." She accepted that. So I tried to keep the cancellation proportional to the instruction rather than letting it grow into a creed.

THERAPIST: That proportionality is a deliberate stance.

AVERY: It felt like a small act of integrity with the present facts rather than surrender to a broader pessimism.

## Part 7
THERAPIST: When you caught yourself about to say the "something always happens" line, what did you do internally before choosing the tentative phrasing?

AVERY: I noticed the automatic sentence forming and imagined it as an old recording in my head. I paused, took one breath, and then tested an alternate sentence out loud to myself: "We can hold this lightly and decide later." That helped. Saying it out loud felt mildly silly, but it interrupted the automatic negative spin enough to let me choose a different path.

THERAPIST: You used a short verbal interruption. How often have you used that technique before?

AVERY: Some; it's one of the cognitive tricks Leah and I have worked with. It's not new, but it's one of the few that can stop me long enough to pick a different phrase. I do that more with texts and drafts than in person. This time I used it in phone calls, which felt more immediate.

THERAPIST: And the result was a more tentative plan.

AVERY: Yes. It turned the phrase "there's no point" into "let's be flexible." Different emotional valence, similar logistical result: dinner might happen, or it might not, but it doesn't become a moral statement about my capacity to enjoy life.

THERAPIST: That's a useful reframe — moving from moral judgment to practical flexibility.

AVERY: It felt less like defeat and more like realistic optimism. Maybe that's a clumsy label, but it's closer to what I want.

THERAPIST: Does that reframe change how you imagine future plans with your father and others?

AVERY: Slightly. I'm starting to imagine plans as layered: initial invitation, confirmation window, backup plan that isn't a narrative of failure. It's administrative, which is a little dry, but it keeps the emotional stakes manageable.

His face changed a little at the idea of a tiny treat, which reminded me that small gestures matter even when plans shift.

THERAPIST: The biscotti triggered a positive response. Did that change your sense of the canceled plan?

AVERY: It did. It made me realize that even if the bookstore and dinner were postponed, there were still small opportunities to make the evening nicer for him. That undercut the "no point" belief; there was point in small things even if the larger plan didn't hold.

THERAPIST: So the distinction between a real disruption and the belief that rest is futile became clearer.

AVERY: Yes. The dizziness was real and warranted caution. My belief that "something always happens" is more about my anticipatory stance. Recognizing the difference helps me choose to reschedule rather than abandon because a specific event doesn't have to define every future plan.

THERAPIST: That sounds like a useful distinction — specific, situational caution versus a sweeping expectation.

AVERY: It is. I can honor the immediate instruction and still plan for future small pleasures without assuming they'll be nullified.

## Part 9

## Part 10
THERAPIST: You mentioned earlier that when you cancel you sometimes feel guilty for disrupting others' time. Did that occur when you called Leah to cancel?

AVERY: A little. I said, "I'm sorry to cancel," and she replied, "It's okay — it's your dad." AVERY: I'd like to avoid the immediate "sorry" as if I'm at fault. Instead, a brief factual statement might be better: "Dad's dizzy; clinic advised rest, so I need to cancel tonight. Can we aim for Friday?" That holds the boundary without loading it with unnecessary shame.

THERAPIST: That phrasing is more neutral.

AVERY: Right. Neutralness feels like a kindness to others and to myself. When I apologize reflexively, I amplify responsibility that isn't solely mine. The neutral language acknowledges the change without creating emotional labor.

THERAPIST: How did Leah handle the neutral phrasing when you tried it?

AVERY: She responded well. When I said the neutral line, she replied, "Okay, Friday works tentatively. Let me know by Thursday." It felt efficient and less emotional. She didn't need my apology; she needed the logistical status.

THERAPIST: That seems to confirm a practical approach works in your network.

AVERY: Yes. It makes me feel less like I'm failing people and more like I'm making choices in response to changing facts. That reduces the corrosive "always happens" narrative.

THERAPIST: Tying decisions to observable indicators seems to ground them in the present.

THERAPIST: That sounds like a flexible repertoire of connection that respects constraints.

THERAPIST: And if a pattern of cancellations persists, what would you want to do differently?
