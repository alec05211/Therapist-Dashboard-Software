SYNTHETIC DEMONSTRATION MATERIAL
Session 01: Intake and stabilization
Participants: Jeremy Heartwell, LCSW; Elena Sadić
Format: Weekly outpatient psychotherapy session, approximately 50 minutes in the fictional timeline
Production note: This is a script source for future text-to-speech generation. All people, events, and clinical material are fictional.

JEREMY: Before we begin, I want you to know that we can set the pace together. You do not need to explain everything perfectly today. What made this feel like the time to reach out?

ELENA: I have been telling myself for a few months that I was just in a busy stretch. I work in legal aid, so there is always a reason that something is urgent. Lately it feels like I am either working, thinking about working, or feeling guilty that I am not working. I am tired all the time, but when I try to rest I feel restless and behind.

JEREMY: It sounds like the pressure follows you even when the workday is over.

ELENA: Yes. I can be sitting on the couch with my partner and be halfway through an email in my head. Then I notice I have not heard what she said, and I feel awful. She has been patient, but I am more irritable than I used to be. Small questions feel like criticism. Then I feel ashamed because she is not actually doing anything wrong.

JEREMY: When you say you are tired all the time, what has that looked like day to day?

ELENA: I stay up late trying to get ahead, but I do not really get ahead. I reread the same document or move things around on a list. Some nights I sleep five hours, some nights more. I skip lunch if I am anxious about a deadline. I used to see friends on Sundays. Now I make plans and then cancel because I cannot imagine having a conversation.

JEREMY: You are still carrying a lot of responsibility, but the ways you have been trying to manage it are taking more from you.

ELENA: That is exactly it. From the outside, I am functioning. I have not missed court. Clients are getting called back. My supervisor says I am doing good work. But I feel like I am holding everything together by staying one step away from falling apart.

JEREMY: What do you imagine would happen if you were not the person who held everything together?

ELENA: Someone would be disappointed. Or they would see that I am not as capable as they think. My family relied on me a lot when my dad got sick last year. It became normal to be the one who remembers appointments and calls people and keeps the tone calm. I do not know how to turn that off.

JEREMY: Being reliable has been important to you, and it may also have become a role that leaves very little room for your own limits.

ELENA: I do not want to be someone who complains. Other people have worse problems. I know that sounds bad.

JEREMY: It sounds like you have a strong rule about what earns care or attention. We can be curious about that rule without deciding today whether it is fair or unfair.

ELENA: I think I would like that. I am tired of arguing with myself about whether I am allowed to be tired.

JEREMY: Earlier, when we spoke briefly by phone, you mentioned that there have been moments when things felt dark enough that you did not want to be here. I want to ask about that directly because your safety matters. What have those moments been like recently?

ELENA: They are not constant. It is more like, after a bad night or after I have disappointed someone, I think, "I wish I could disappear for a while." A few times I have thought that people might be better off if I were not making everything harder. Then I get scared that I even had the thought.

JEREMY: I appreciate you telling me. When those thoughts show up, have you found yourself making a plan to hurt yourself or feeling that you might act on them?

ELENA: No. I do not have a plan, and I do not want to die. I want the pressure to stop. The thought makes me feel ashamed, so I usually try to push it away and act normal.

JEREMY: That distinction is important. Wanting relief is not the same as wanting to end your life, and we should still take the moments seriously. What has helped you get through them so far?

ELENA: My partner, Nora, even when I have not told her the whole truth. My dog gets me out of bed. Sometimes I call my sister. And honestly, work can distract me, although I know that is not always helping.

JEREMY: Those are meaningful connections. For this first week, I would like us to make a simple plan for what you will do if the thoughts become more frequent, feel harder to set aside, or change in a way that worries you. Would it be all right to identify a few people and resources now?

ELENA: Yes. I think it would be a relief to have words for it instead of pretending it is not happening.

JEREMY: We can write down Nora and your sister as people you could tell directly that you are having a hard moment. We can also include the local crisis line, emergency services, and this office's after-hours contact information. The point is not that you have done something wrong. It is to make reaching for support easier if you need it.

ELENA: That makes sense. I do not know if I am ready to tell Nora everything tonight, but I could say I have been struggling more than she knows.

JEREMY: That sounds like a realistic first step. We can revisit how that conversation goes rather than asking you to disclose more than you are ready for all at once.

ELENA: I appreciate that. People usually either get very alarmed or tell me to take a vacation. Neither one feels possible.

JEREMY: We are looking for something smaller and more usable. When you notice the pressure building, what do you tend to feel in your body first?

ELENA: My shoulders get tight. I hold my breath. I start clicking between tabs and cannot decide what to do first.

JEREMY: Could those be signals to pause before the day has become unmanageable? Not to solve the workload, but to check what is happening.

ELENA: Maybe. I usually treat those signs as proof that I need to work faster.

JEREMY: What might a two-minute pause look like if it did not have to earn its place?

ELENA: I could step outside the building. Or put both feet on the floor and drink water before I answer another message. It sounds almost too small.

JEREMY: Small is useful when the alternative has been waiting until you are completely depleted. Would you be willing to notice one pressure moment this week and write down three things: what happened, the thought that arrived, and what your body was doing?

ELENA: Yes. I can do that. I do not want it to become another assignment I fail.

JEREMY: Let us make it an observation, not a scorecard. One example is enough. I also wonder whether there is one thing that has felt like you outside of work recently.

ELENA: I used to walk the dog by the river early on Saturday. I would get coffee and not look at my phone. I have not done it in weeks.

JEREMY: Would returning to that be possible in a small way this Saturday? Not as a reward for productivity, but as information about what helps you feel connected to yourself.

ELENA: I could do twenty minutes. I think Nora would come with me if I asked.

JEREMY: That sounds like a good experiment. Before we finish, I want to check how you are feeling after talking about the harder material today.

ELENA: Lighter, actually. Still embarrassed, but less alone with it. I did not expect that.

JEREMY: You were honest about something difficult, and you do not have to carry it by yourself here. Between now and next week, your plan is to notice one pressure cycle, try the Saturday walk, and tell Nora that you have been struggling more than she knows. If your safety feels different or more urgent, you will use the support plan we outlined rather than handling it alone. Does that fit?

ELENA: Yes. It feels manageable when you say it that way.

JEREMY: Good. We will pick up there next week and see what you noticed.


GENERAL SESSION SUMMARY

Elena described several months of escalating work-related strain despite maintaining outward responsibilities. She linked late work, inconsistent sleep, skipped meals, social withdrawal, and increased irritability with a persistent fear of disappointing others or being seen as incapable. Family responsibilities during her father's illness reinforced a long-standing role as the person who stays composed and handles practical needs.

Elena disclosed intermittent passive thoughts of disappearing or others being better off without her during periods of overwhelm. She denied a plan, intent, or desire to die and described wanting relief from pressure. Jeremy responded with direct, calm assessment; identified existing supports including her partner, sister, and dog; and collaboratively outlined a safety plan that includes direct contact with supports, crisis resources, emergency services, and the practice's after-hours contact information if her experience changes or becomes more urgent.

The session established an initial treatment focus on recognizing the cycle of uncertainty, self-criticism, overwork, and depletion. Elena agreed to observe one pressure moment by noting the situation, automatic thought, and physical cues; take a brief Saturday walk with her dog; and tell her partner that she has been struggling more than she has shared. The next session should review what was observable, how the conversation with Nora went, and whether Elena's safety status changed.
